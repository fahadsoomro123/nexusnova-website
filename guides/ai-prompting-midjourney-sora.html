A Practical Prompting Workflow for Midjourney Images and Sora Video

A source-linked workflow for turning an image or video idea into a clearer, more testable creative brief.

Start with a visible outcome, not a pile of adjectives

A prompt is most useful when it describes a shot a person could imagine directing. Start with the subject, its action, where it is, and what the viewer should notice first. “A small glass greenhouse in a windswept coastal garden at dawn, viewed through rain-speckled glass” creates a visual plan. “Beautiful, cinematic, amazing, ultra-detailed” does not tell the model which details matter or how to arrange them. Before adding style, write the one-sentence intent and decide whether the output is a product image, establishing frame, portrait, storyboard panel or moving sequence.

Those jobs have different requirements. A product image rewards a clean silhouette, controlled reflections and space for a label. A video needs an action and a beginning-to-end change. Use the [AI Prompt Enhancer](../ai-prompt-enhancer.html) to separate these choices instead of rewriting everything at once. It organizes text locally and does not call a generative model, so its output should be a brief you can review, not an automatic quality upgrade.

Separate subject, scene, composition and style

Treat a prompt as coordinated instructions: subject and action, environment, framing, lighting, palette, texture, mood and constraints. Put the main information early, then add only details that improve the intended result. A close-up portrait and a wide architectural shot should not share the same camera language. A minimal editorial image normally benefits from a restrained palette and negative space rather than a long list of competing aesthetics. The objective is not maximum prompt length; it is a hierarchy of details the viewer must perceive.

Composition is often left implicit. Specify wide, medium or close framing, subject placement, foreground elements, background depth and where attention should go. If a cover image needs room for a headline, say that the subject sits to one side and the opposite side remains uncluttered. That instruction does not guarantee perfect placement, but it gives you an acceptance criterion. Avoid contradictions such as “perfectly centered with lots of empty space on the left” unless you explain which element is centered and which space must remain open.

Write for Midjourney’s still-image workflow

Midjourney starts with a text description of the image you want. Its official [Prompt Basics documentation](https://docs.midjourney.com/hc/en-us/articles/32023408776205-Prompt-Basics) explains how subject, medium, environment, lighting, color, mood and composition shape a brief. It also notes that concise prompts can work well, while more detail can provide extra control when specific elements matter. Apply that as a practical principle: keep the essentials, then add only constraints that solve a visible problem.

Aspect ratio is a separate decision from subject description. The official [Midjourney documentation index](https://docs.midjourney.com/hc/en-us/sections/32013365472909-Prompting-Basics) covers parameters such as “--ar”. Plan the final placement before generating: square for a profile tile, portrait for a poster-like asset, or wide for a header. Reuse a baseline prompt and change one variable at a time, such as camera distance or lighting. An image may become more attractive yet less useful for the slot it must fill, so evaluate at the real publication size, not only a large preview.

Write for Sora’s temporal workflow

A video prompt needs time. Describe what happens first, what changes, and the shot’s final state. “A cyclist rides through a city” leaves considerable freedom. “A cyclist enters from the left, passes wet shopfronts, then slows beside a red bus as the camera tracks at walking speed” describes an action and camera relationship. It does not guarantee exact motion, but gives you a stronger brief to evaluate. Use the [OpenAI Help Center](https://help.openai.com/) for current Sora instructions and controls because interfaces and model behavior change.

Separate subject movement from camera movement. A subject may walk toward the lens while the camera remains locked; a vehicle may move while the camera pans; or the camera may pass a stationary object. If both are moving, describe their relationship. Add sound or dialogue only when necessary. Do not depend on generated in-frame text for essential product labels, legal wording or exact brand copy. Put critical text in an editor and verify spelling, contrast and safe placement before publishing.

Use camera and lighting words only when they clarify

Camera language is useful when it narrows the intended result. “Locked-off wide shot”, “slow push-in”, “overhead tabletop view” and “handheld follow shot” imply different experiences. A camera note should support the story, not sound technical for its own sake. If a term is unfamiliar, describe the viewer’s experience instead: the subject fills the frame, the camera slowly approaches, or the scene is seen from above. Choose one primary viewpoint and combine movements only when they can plausibly occur together.

Lighting can anchor tone and improve continuity. Describe the source and quality when they matter: soft window light, late-afternoon backlight, a narrow strip of neon or a high-contrast studio key. Avoid mixing conditions that fight unless the contrast is intentional. If several images or clips must match, reuse a compact continuity note for clothing, palette, setting and light direction, then vary the action or framing. Generated output remains probabilistic, so continuity notes improve the brief but cannot replace visual review.

Make iteration a controlled experiment

Create a baseline with the subject, scene, framing and one style direction. Inspect the result against four questions: is the subject identifiable, is the focal point where it should be, is the scene plausible, and does the asset communicate the idea? Change the weakest dimension only. If composition is wrong, do not simultaneously rewrite lighting, palette, camera, mood and subject. A small controlled iteration teaches you something; rewriting everything produces a new result without revealing why it changed.

Save the exact prompt, platform, settings and a short note about what worked. For video, include shot structure or duration when available. For images, record aspect ratio and style settings. This creates a reusable prompt library rather than a folder of unexplained outputs. Revisit notes after model updates because behavior can change. The platform’s current documentation takes priority over saved prompts from months earlier. When a prompt fails, record the failure specifically, such as “subject hidden by clutter”, rather than a vague rating such as “bad”.

Rights, privacy and production checks

Too many unrelated style words can create a muddled direction. Choose one primary visual language and a couple of supporting qualities. Conflicting camera instructions make action hard to parse; specify one primary angle and movement at a time. Abstract language can express mood, but pair it with visible cues. “Nostalgic” becomes more actionable when paired with warm faded color, soft halation, period-appropriate props and restrained contrast. Do not use generative tools to impersonate a real person, bypass consent or create misleading footage meant to be presented as authentic evidence.

For commercial projects, check rights to reference materials, likeness permissions, platform rules and disclosure obligations. Keep confidential client or personal information out of prompts unless you have permission and have reviewed the service’s data handling. Check the final asset at the target size, inspect mobile crops and leave safe space for captions and platform controls. The fastest workflow is not the longest prompt; it is the one that makes each generation answer a clear question and keeps acceptance criteria visible.
