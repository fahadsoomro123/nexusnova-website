How to Set a Freelance Hourly Rate and Quote Remote Projects

Build a sustainable freelance rate from realistic billable capacity, overhead, reserves, project scope, contingency and delivery risk.

A sustainable quote starts with the hours you can actually bill, the costs you need to cover and the scope you are agreeing to deliver. Work through those numbers before sending a price to a client.

Start from business economics, not an online rate list

A freelance rate must pay for visible client work and the less visible work needed to deliver it. Competitor research can help you understand a market range, but it cannot tell you whether a price covers software, insurance, unpaid sales calls, revisions, training, pension contributions, equipment replacement or time off. Copying a senior specialist’s advertised rate without understanding scope can be as unhelpful as underpricing a complex project just to win it.

Build a yearly model first. Start with the income you want to retain, add annual business costs, choose a realistic reserve for taxes and benefits, and divide the required revenue by believable billable hours. This turns a rate from a guess into a set of visible assumptions. Revisit the assumptions when your pipeline, experience or costs change. The calculation does not dictate the market price; it shows the economics the business needs to support.

Estimate billable capacity realistically

A calendar contains 52 weeks, but a freelancer rarely bills all 52 at full capacity. Holidays, illness, training, proposals, invoicing, project management, quiet periods and business development all consume time. Someone available for 40 hours per week may bill far fewer than 2,080 annual hours. A model using every available hour quietly assumes the business requires no administration and that there are never gaps between projects.

Estimate weekly billable hours and billable weeks separately. For example, 25 billable hours across 46 weeks creates 1,150 annual billable hours. The remaining hours are not waste; they are part of the operating cost of selling and delivering the service. If clients require frequent meetings or revisions, reduce the planned billable capacity instead of hiding that labor in optimistic arithmetic. Use timesheets to replace estimates with evidence where possible.

Calculate the annual revenue requirement

List software, equipment replacement, internet, workspace, accountant, professional memberships, insurance, payment processing, subcontractors and other recurring expenses. Separate one-time investments from ongoing costs and include a reasonable replacement allowance for essential equipment. Decide which expenses are included in your rate, quoted separately or reimbursed by the client. Avoid counting a reimbursed purchase twice in the pricing model.

Suppose the annual income target is $60,000 and overhead is $6,000. Before a tax or benefits reserve, the revenue requirement is $66,000. If the model reserves 25% of gross revenue, the target becomes $66,000 divided by 0.75, or $88,000. This is a planning convention, not a tax calculation. The correct reserve depends on jurisdiction, entity, deductions and personal facts. Use professional advice instead of treating one percentage as tax law.

Divide by hours to create a planning floor

With an $88,000 revenue target and 1,150 billable hours, the planning floor is roughly $76.52 an hour. The result reflects only the inputs above. It is not automatically the market rate or the amount every client should pay. A specialist who delivers measurable business value may price above the floor; a constrained budget may require a narrower scope, phased delivery or a different service package.

Compare project offers with the estimated rate and the amount of work actually required. A fixed-fee project that appears to pay $100 an hour can turn into $50 an hour if revision work doubles. Track direct expenses and actual hours after delivery. Over several projects, you can identify profitable services, chronic under-scoping and tasks that should be repriced or discontinued. A rate calculation is strongest when it is paired with records from real delivery.

Write the quote around outcomes and scope boundaries

A good quote identifies the result, deliverables, exclusions, assumptions, client responsibilities, milestones, delivery format, deadlines and acceptance criteria. “Build a website” is not precise. “Deliver five responsive pages from approved copy and supplied branding, with two revision rounds and an agreed browser support list” can be estimated and reviewed. A written scope protects both sides by giving them a reference point when expectations change.

Break work into research, planning, production, quality review, client coordination and handover. Include testing and final delivery. If uncertainty is high, quote a range or run a paid discovery phase rather than pretending unknowns have disappeared. Specify the feedback window, the materials the client must supply and what triggers a change order. Clear scope is not bureaucracy for its own sake; it links the promised outcome to the resources required to produce it.

Use contingency for real uncertainty

A fixed-fee quote can be modeled as estimated hours multiplied by the planning rate, plus an explicit contingency and project-specific expenses. For 18 hours at $76.52, labor is approximately $1,377. A 15% contingency adds about $207 before direct costs. Round the final figure sensibly and align it with scope, value and commercial context. Explain whether the amount is a fixed price, a cap or an estimate that needs further approval.

Contingency should reflect known uncertainty, such as undocumented legacy systems, unclear source assets, third-party dependencies or approval delays. It is not an unexplained markup. State revision limits, out-of-scope hourly rates, payment milestones and what happens if required assets arrive late. Compare actual time against estimated time after the project closes. That feedback gives you a basis to refine future contingencies rather than simply increasing every quote after one difficult job.

Choose the right pricing structure

Hourly pricing can fit evolving requirements or flexible support. Fixed-fee pricing fits defined deliverables, stable acceptance criteria and manageable dependencies. Retainers can reserve capacity for repeat work, but they need clear limits on response times, unused hours and included services. Value-based pricing may make sense when client outcomes can be evidenced, but it requires credible value assumptions and a contract that does not guarantee factors beyond your control.

No model eliminates risk. Fixed fees transfer estimation risk to the provider, while hourly work can leave the client uncertain about the total unless checkpoints or a cap are agreed. Explain trade-offs plainly and choose a structure that supports delivery. If a buyer cannot afford the full scope, reduce deliverables or sequence milestones rather than silently accepting the same work at a loss. Pricing should describe the deal both parties can realistically perform.

Protect cash flow and professional boundaries

A profitable quote can still create a cash-flow problem if payment arrives months later. Define deposits or milestones, invoice promptly, and state what happens when approvals or payment are delayed. Obtain written approval for out-of-scope work before beginning it. Clarify deliverable ownership and rights transfer, especially where source files, stock media, licenses or reusable templates are involved. A short, consistent contract template reduces misunderstandings across projects.

Do not treat a tax reserve as a substitute for a cash-flow plan. Taxes, platform fees, refunds, chargebacks, currency conversions and uneven demand can affect the money available to spend. Maintain a buffer appropriate to the business risk and its obligations. Check local rules for registration, invoicing, consumer rights and record retention. A simple forecast and an understandable contract are more useful than an elaborate spreadsheet built on unsupported assumptions.

Improve estimates with time tracking and review

Track work by category, not only by client. Research, meetings, production, corrections, communication and handover can have different patterns. Compare estimated and actual hours and record why they diverged: new requirements, slow approvals, technical surprises, underestimated testing or unusually efficient delivery. After several projects, recurring problems become visible enough to change the template, contingency or onboarding questions.

Review rates monthly or quarterly. If every project requires extra review time, make revision rounds explicit. If small clients create heavy support overhead, consider a minimum engagement fee or scoped starter package. If a service repeatedly delivers more value than expected, consider packaged pricing. Use several projects of evidence rather than reacting to one good or bad result. Keep client and financial records in line with confidentiality and reporting obligations.

Turn the calculation into a quoting workflow

First set an annual income target and list overhead. Next choose billable weeks and hours that reflect your calendar. Choose a reserve percentage after considering local professional advice, compute the rate floor, estimate each project task, then add justified direct costs and contingency. Finally write the deliverables, exclusions, milestones, revision rounds and payment terms into the client proposal. The sequence forces assumptions into the open before price becomes a promise.

Use the [Freelance Rate & Project Quote Calculator](/freelance-rate-calculator.html) to run capacity scenarios. See what happens if billable hours drop, overhead rises or a project takes longer than planned. Keep the result as a planning note, then prepare a client-ready quote with the relevant contract terms. Sustainable pricing is not about choosing the largest imaginable number. It is about making business economics visible so you can deliver consistent work without discovering that the margin vanished after delivery.
