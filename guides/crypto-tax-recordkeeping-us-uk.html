Crypto Tax Records and Gain Estimates: US and UK Planning Guide

Understand the transaction records behind a crypto gain estimate and where simplified US and UK rules differ.

Open the matching free tool →

A portfolio profit number is not the same as a tax bill

A portfolio screen usually shows changes in market value. Tax calculations generally start from a disposal event and the relevant cost basis, proceeds and allowable costs, then apply the rules for a taxpayer’s jurisdiction. Selling a token, exchanging it for another token or using it to pay for something may count as a disposal even when no bank withdrawal occurs. Receiving tokens as compensation, mining income or staking rewards can follow different rules from selling an asset already held as an investment.

The [Crypto Tax & Profit/Loss Estimator](../crypto-tax-estimator.html) separates a rough disposal gain or loss from a simplified tax illustration. It cannot determine every classification, relief, pooling rule or personal circumstance. Treat the output as a record-checking aid, not a number to copy directly to a return. A plausible arithmetic result can still be tax-law wrong if the event, acquisition basis, tax year or activity is classified incorrectly.

Keep transaction-level source records

For each event, preserve the timestamp, asset, units, transaction ID, wallet or exchange, value in the required local currency, fees and a note explaining the event. Save exchange statements and the conversion method used for prices reported in another currency. A transfer between wallets you own may not be a disposal, but records help distinguish a transfer from a sale. Reconcile movements so the same asset is not counted as disposed in one wallet and newly acquired in another.

Cost basis gets difficult when assets move across exchanges, wallets and bridges. Keep original acquisition records rather than relying only on the current balance shown by one platform. If an exchange closes or restricts access to old exports, your own saved records become especially valuable. Document gaps and assumptions instead of silently replacing missing values with guesses. Store records securely and limit access, because transaction history can reveal holdings, spending patterns and links among addresses that you may prefer to keep private.

United States: classify the transaction first

The IRS treats digital assets as property for federal tax purposes. Disposals may create capital gain or loss, while some receipts can be income. The IRS [digital assets guidance](https://www.irs.gov/filing/digital-assets) explains common transaction types and tax-return questions. Certain brokers report covered digital-asset proceeds on Form 1099-DA for relevant transactions beginning with 2025; the IRS also publishes an [explanation of Form 1099-DA](https://www.irs.gov/businesses/understanding-your-form-1099-da). A broker form does not replace the taxpayer’s duty to report transactions accurately.

For a simplified 2026 federal illustration, long-term capital-gain treatment can depend on filing status and taxable income; short-term gains are generally considered with ordinary income. State taxes, net investment income tax, carryovers, special assets, business activity and income from staking or rewards are outside this compact estimator. If several wallets or exchanges are involved, reconcile them before estimating. A tool that uses one proceeds, one basis and one income field cannot reconstruct every trade, identify every taxable event or determine all facts on a federal return.

United Kingdom: disposal, pooling and matching rules matter

HMRC explains that individuals may need to calculate Capital Gains Tax when they sell cryptoassets, exchange them for a different type of token, use them to pay for goods or services, or make other disposals covered by the rules. The official [cryptoasset disposal guidance](https://www.gov.uk/guidance/check-if-you-need-to-pay-tax-when-you-sell-cryptoassets) describes what records to keep and how to work out gains. UK matching rules can apply differently to same-day acquisitions, purchases within 30 days and pooled holdings. A single purchase-price input is therefore not sufficient for every portfolio.

For the 2026–27 illustration, the estimator applies an assumed £3,000 individual annual exempt amount and illustrates rates of 18 percent within a remaining basic-rate band and 24 percent above it. Review HMRC’s official [Capital Gains Tax rates and allowances](https://www.gov.uk/guidance/capital-gains-tax-rates-and-allowances) page for the applicable year. Your income, losses, residence, reliefs, activity and type of asset can change the result. Company holdings, trusts and income-type receipts need separate handling rather than using an individual’s simplified gain calculator.

Calculate proceeds, basis and fees consistently

The basic arithmetic is disposal proceeds minus cost basis minus eligible disposal costs. If an asset was sold for $9,000 with a basis of $6,500 and $100 of eligible selling fees, the simple gain before other losses is $2,400. The example is only as reliable as the valuation and basis records behind it. Network fees, acquisition fees and other transaction costs may be treated differently under the applicable rules, so do not assume every charge on an exchange statement is deductible in every situation.

Make currency conversion explicit. A UK taxpayer may need pound values for each transaction date even if an exchange reports dollars. A US taxpayer may have to calculate dollar values from foreign-currency records. Record the source, date and method so another person can reproduce the figure. Converting only the final total can be misleading when the exchange rate differs between acquisition and disposal. Do not mix currencies in one calculation, and confirm the way fees are treated for the relevant asset and jurisdiction.

Realized losses and annual allowances need documentation

The calculator accepts one field for other eligible realized losses to demonstrate their potential effect, but actual treatment depends on recognition rules, carryovers and the order in which losses or allowances apply. Do not enter a portfolio’s unrealized decline as if it were automatically a deductible loss. A token falling in price on a dashboard does not necessarily mean a taxable disposal has happened. Preserve evidence of the transaction that created each loss and track carryovers between tax years where relevant.

In the UK, an individual annual exempt amount applies subject to the applicable rules and is not the same as a general deduction for any loss in a wallet. In the US, capital loss netting and carryovers can affect the return, including limits in situations where losses exceed gains. Tokens that are lost, stolen, frozen or inaccessible need specific analysis; they should not automatically be entered as an ordinary sale with zero proceeds. Ask a qualified professional when the classification or amount is material.

Separate income events from later asset sales

Rewards, staking, mining, airdrops, employment payments and business receipts may be treated differently from capital gains on an investment disposal. If a receipt is taxable income at its market value when received, that receipt may form part of the cost basis used when the asset is sold later, subject to the rules that apply. Failing to record the original receipt can make the later gain look much larger or smaller than it should be. Keep income events separate from sale and exchange records.

The [IRS digital assets page](https://www.irs.gov/filing/digital-assets) and HMRC’s [cryptoassets guidance](https://www.gov.uk/guidance/check-if-you-need-to-pay-tax-when-you-sell-cryptoassets) provide starting points for official treatment. Both systems distinguish types of transactions and include detailed conditions. A calculator that only asks for proceeds, basis, fees and losses intentionally does not classify every receipt. Use it after transaction classification, not as a substitute for that work.

Reconcile the year before filing

At year end, reconcile starting balances, purchases, disposals, transfers, rewards and ending balances across every exchange and self-custody wallet. Investigate negative balances, unexplained inflows and duplicate trade rows. Match transaction counts to source statements and make sure fiat deposits and withdrawals are not mistaken for token purchases or sales. Preserve original exports and store cleaned spreadsheets separately so a reviewer can reproduce the transformation and trace a total to a source row.

Keep an assumptions note beside every estimate: tax year, residency, filing status, whether fees are included, exchange-rate method, how losses were allocated, and which transactions need review. This helps an adviser identify unresolved items and prevents a rounded output from being mistaken for an official calculation. If the amount is material or your records involve multiple jurisdictions, obtain qualified tax advice before filing. The point of an estimator is to expose missing information early, not to remove the need for it.

Sources and further reading

Start with the [IRS digital-assets guidance](https://www.irs.gov/filing/digital-assets), the IRS [Form 1099-DA explanation](https://www.irs.gov/businesses/understanding-your-form-1099-da), HMRC’s [cryptoasset disposal guidance](https://www.gov.uk/guidance/check-if-you-need-to-pay-tax-when-you-sell-cryptoassets) and [Capital Gains Tax rates and allowances](https://www.gov.uk/guidance/capital-gains-tax-rates-and-allowances). Rules and reporting requirements can change, so confirm the current year on the official page.
