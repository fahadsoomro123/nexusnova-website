Privacy Policies and Terms for SaaS and Mobile Apps: A Practical Checklist

Map real data flows, user promises, vendors, retention and service terms before drafting legal documents.

Open the matching free tool →

Start with what the product actually does

A privacy policy should describe real product behavior, not what the founder hopes an app does. Start with a data inventory: what users type, what the device automatically sends, what gets stored, which vendors receive it and why. Categories might include account details, support messages, diagnostics, analytics identifiers, payment metadata, uploaded files, location or device permissions, but include only those that actually apply. If a feature does not collect a data type, do not leave boilerplate claiming it does.

The [Privacy Policy & Terms Generator](../privacy-policy-terms-generator.html) creates an editable first draft from declared practices. It cannot inspect source code, SDKs, databases, backups, vendor contracts or retention settings. Compare every statement against a real data-flow map and a release build. A generated sentence can become a promise the company is expected to keep, so drafting should make verification easier rather than substitute for it.

Map collection, purpose and sharing

For each data type, record the purpose, collection point, storage location, retention period, access roles and recipient. “We use data to improve our services” may be too broad if the product sends diagnostics to third parties or uses advertising identifiers. Explain the practical purpose in plain language, and distinguish required operations, optional analytics, marketing, payment processing and legal recordkeeping. If you rely on consent or another legal basis, review whether that approach fits the relevant market and the type of information involved.

The FTC’s [mobile-app guidance](https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/marketing-your-mobile-app-get-it-right-start) recommends transparency and privacy protections built into design. It is a useful US baseline, not a complete global legal analysis. Create a vendor inventory from the code and procurement records, not memory. Check hosting, authentication, analytics, crash reporting, email delivery, customer support, payment processing and advertising. Do not copy an unrelated provider list or promise “we do not share data” without checking technical behavior and contracts.

Describe third-party tools precisely

Apps often depend on cloud hosting, analytics, crash reporting, authentication, payment processors, email delivery, push notifications, customer support and advertising. Identify the actual SDKs and services used in each release. Confirm what data each receives, whether it acts as a processor or independent party under the relevant contract, where processing occurs and how retention works. The policy should describe the relationship accurately and link to relevant vendor privacy notices when that helps users understand the arrangement.

Do not say that information stays on a device if sync, crash reporting, analytics or payment operations send data to a server. Equally, do not claim that every piece of user content is collected when a tool processes files locally. Distinguish on-device processing from server-side processing and explain whether the data leaves the device. Repeat the data map after adding an SDK or feature; a policy that was accurate at launch can become incorrect after routine product changes.

Retention, deletion and user controls

Retention language must match operational reality. Identify how long records are kept or the criteria for deciding, and consider backups, security logs, fraud prevention and statutory accounting records. If a user deletes an account, clarify which records are removed, which persist temporarily in backups and which must be retained for a valid reason. Avoid promising instantaneous permanent deletion if backups, legal obligations or third-party processors mean the real process takes longer.

Provide practical routes for access, correction, deletion, export, objection or consent withdrawal where those rights apply. Explain how to contact the team and what verification is necessary, but do not demand more information than a request needs. If the app has privacy settings, tell users where to find them. A rights section only helps if support has a documented process and can meet the deadlines that apply. Test the workflow before promising it in public copy.

Cookies, analytics, advertising and consent

Inventory cookies, local storage, pixels, mobile advertising IDs and server-side analytics. Explain each purpose and how choices are stored. Consent requirements differ by jurisdiction and processing type, so one checkbox may not meet every obligation. Do not say that rejecting optional analytics disables all collection if essential security logs remain. Conversely, do not label optional tracking “strictly necessary” just because it helps a business dashboard. The consent language, policy and technical configuration should agree.

Test real choices in a clean browser or app profile. Reject optional analytics, navigate key flows and inspect whether optional events still fire. Test operating-system permissions and a persistent privacy-settings control. The policy, consent screen, SDK configuration and backend behavior are one system. If they contradict each other, rewriting the document alone will not fix the issue. For the website or app, make sure people can find privacy information before they create an account or submit personal information.

Terms should explain the service relationship

Terms of service describe what the product provides, what users may do and what happens when either side does not meet expectations. Depending on the service, cover eligibility, account responsibilities, acceptable use, user content, intellectual-property rights, subscriptions, trials, renewal, cancellation, refunds, availability, support, service changes and termination. A free utility may need a simpler agreement than a paid subscription, but it still benefits from clear boundaries and an honest description of the product.

Avoid inserting aggressive clauses copied from another company. Refund and cancellation language must match the actual checkout and support process and cannot remove mandatory consumer rights. Liability wording, governing law, arbitration or dispute clauses can be restricted by applicable law. The generator can provide an editable structure, but a qualified lawyer should review provisions that allocate meaningful commercial risk or serve users in multiple countries. Terms should agree with the privacy policy, checkout screen, marketing copy and actual feature behavior.

Children, sensitive data and security promises

If children may use the app or it handles sensitive information, review the project more carefully. Determine whether age assurance, parental consent, special notices, restrictions on targeting or safeguards apply. Health, financial, precise-location, biometric and identity data can trigger additional obligations or higher user expectations. Do not claim that an app is “child safe” or “compliant worldwide” simply because a generated policy has a section about children. Check the intended audience, platform requirements and actual data collection.

Describe security measures only if they are implemented and can be substantiated. Access controls, encryption, backups, security testing and incident response need evidence. Absolute language such as “unhackable” or “100% secure” is not credible. Review app-store privacy declarations, permissions prompts, website policy and backend behavior as one set. If a feature starts processing a sensitive category, repeat the data mapping exercise and review legal basis, consent, retention, vendor terms and incident-handling arrangements.

A release checklist for generated documents

Before publishing a generated policy or terms document, have the product owner and engineering lead verify each claim against a current data-flow map. Check company name, product name, contact address, jurisdiction, effective date, support route, vendor list, retention language, deletion workflow, payment terms and app-store disclosures. Replace every placeholder and remove sections that assert behavior the product does not have. Save a reviewed version with the release record so the team can explain what changed.

Review the rendered document on mobile, confirm headings and links work, and make it easy to find before account creation or data submission. Set a reminder to review the notice whenever a new analytics SDK, payment vendor, account flow or data category is introduced. A policy update may require coordinated changes to product UX, consent screens, support instructions and user notices. The aim is not a longer document; it is a clear, current description that matches the product and that the organization can honor.

Sources and further reading

Read the FTC’s [mobile-app guidance](https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/marketing-your-mobile-app-get-it-right-start) and its [technology privacy and security resources](https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/privacy-security/tech). For a product used in several regions, consult relevant official regulators and qualified counsel on the laws and contractual duties that apply.
