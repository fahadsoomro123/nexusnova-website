What is a weighted decision matrix?

A weighted decision matrix is a structured way to compare several options against the same set of criteria. You first decide what matters, assign an importance weight to each criterion, then score every option using the same scale. The calculator multiplies each score by the criterion's share of the total weight and adds the results.

Decision matrix formula

For each option, the weighted score is the sum of criterion score × normalized criterion weight. With a 1–10 scoring scale, the final result also stays on a 1–10 scale. For example, if price has a weight of 40 and support has a weight of 20, price influences the result twice as strongly as support.

How to use the calculator

  1. Name at least two options you are genuinely considering.
  2. Add criteria that matter to the decision, such as cost, reliability, features, travel time or ease of use.
  3. Give each criterion a weight greater than zero. Use larger numbers for more important criteria.
  4. Score every option from 1 to 10 using the same meaning for every row.
  5. Calculate the ranking, then inspect why the winner scored well instead of treating the top number as an automatic answer.

Handle costs and other “lower is better” criteria carefully

The calculator assumes a higher score always means a better fit. For a cost criterion, convert the raw price into a preference score first: the most affordable acceptable option might receive 9 or 10, while an expensive option might receive a lower score. Do the same for travel time, risk, energy use or any other measure where a smaller raw number is preferable.

Avoid false precision

A matrix makes your assumptions visible; it does not make subjective judgments objectively true. If two options finish very close together, try changing uncertain weights or scores by one point. If the winner changes easily, the decision is sensitive and you should gather better information before relying on the ranking.

Good uses for a decision matrix

  • Comparing software plans, vendors or devices with the same requirements.
  • Shortlisting project ideas, features or content topics.
  • Choosing between travel, study or work options when several trade-offs matter.
  • Making a team discussion more transparent by writing down criteria before debating the winner.

When not to rely on the score alone

Do not use a simple weighted matrix as the sole basis for medical, legal, safety-critical, investment or other high-stakes decisions. Important constraints can be binary rather than gradual: an option that fails a mandatory requirement may need to be excluded even if its weighted score is high.

Weighted decisions need visible assumptions

A decision matrix is useful when several choices must be compared against criteria that do not have equal importance. Weights represent relative importance and scores represent the evaluator's judgement, so the final ranking is a structured summary of assumptions rather than a universal truth.

The calculator normalizes weights proportionally when their total is not exactly 100. This preserves the relative importance you entered but means a weight change can affect every option's final score.

Run a sensitivity check

Change uncertain weights or scores and see whether the result moves materially. When a result is sensitive to small plausible changes, record the assumptions and investigate the uncertainty before relying on the ranking.

Decision-matrix questions

Should every criterion have the same weight?

Only if every criterion matters equally to the decision. Weighting should reflect the priorities agreed before scoring the options, not be adjusted after seeing which option wins.

How should I score a cost or risk?

First define whether a high value is good or bad. Reverse the scoring direction for criteria where lower is better, and use the same scale for every option.

Does the highest total prove an option is best?

No. The result depends on chosen criteria, weights and judgement-based scores. Use the ranking to organise a discussion, then review trade-offs and assumptions before deciding.
