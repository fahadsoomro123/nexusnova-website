Post-Quantum Encryption: Why the Web Is Upgrading Before Quantum Computers Arrive

Published 23 August 2026•NexusNova Editorial Team

Large quantum computers capable of breaking widely used public-key cryptography do not exist today. So why are browsers, networks and security companies already changing encryption? Because some information needs to stay secret for years.

The problem with waiting

An attacker can record encrypted traffic today and save it. If future technology later makes today's cryptography breakable, the attacker could try to decrypt those old recordings. Security engineers call this “harvest now, decrypt later.”

That creates a timing problem. If data needs to remain confidential for a decade, upgrading only after a powerful quantum computer appears would be too late for traffic already captured.

What “post-quantum” means

Post-quantum cryptography uses algorithms designed to resist attacks from both classical and future quantum computers. These algorithms run on ordinary computers; you do not need a quantum machine to use them.

In 2024, NIST finalized major post-quantum standards including ML-KEM for key establishment and ML-DSA for digital signatures. The web industry has since been working on deployment and interoperability.

Why hybrid encryption is common during migration

Rather than replacing established cryptography in one jump, many systems combine a classical algorithm with a post-quantum one. The connection is designed to remain protected if at least one of the components stays secure. This hybrid approach reduces the risk of betting everything on a newly deployed algorithm while still gaining quantum-resistant protection.

Is this already happening on normal web traffic?

Yes. Cloudflare reported in 2026 that a large majority of human-generated TLS traffic through its network was already using post-quantum protection for key agreement. That does not mean every part of HTTPS is fully quantum-resistant yet. Encryption/key exchange and authentication/certificates are separate migration problems.

Encryption versus authentication

Encryption protects the secrecy of the connection. Authentication helps your browser prove it is talking to the real website. Post-quantum key exchange has moved quickly, while replacing certificate signatures and the broader authentication ecosystem is a larger compatibility challenge.

Will users notice?

Ideally, no. A successful cryptographic migration happens underneath normal browsing. The challenge for browser and network engineers is to introduce stronger algorithms without making connections slow, unreliable or incompatible with existing infrastructure.

Should ordinary users do anything?

There is usually no special switch to enable. Keeping browsers and operating systems updated is the useful action because support for new security protocols arrives through software updates. Organizations that manage long-lived sensitive data have a much larger planning job because they need to inventory systems, certificates, VPNs, libraries and cryptographic dependencies.

Does quantum computing mean current encryption is broken?

No. It is important not to turn a migration plan into panic. The industry is upgrading precisely because responsible security planning happens before the emergency. Current quantum computers are not known to be capable of breaking the widely deployed public-key systems at practical Internet scale.

Bottom line

Post-quantum security is a rare technology transition where the future threat changes what should be protected today. The work now happening in TLS, browsers and network infrastructure is an attempt to make the change gradual, interoperable and boring for end users—which is exactly what good infrastructure work should feel like.
