UKRAINE / 22 AUGUST 2026

Kryvyi Rih strike deepens the civilian toll as diplomacy remains stuck

A deadly Russian drone attack on a shopping centre has sharpened attention on civilian risk just as both sides expand long-range strikes and formal peace efforts show little momentum.

Published 22 Aug 2026NexusNova Editorial DeskApprox. 6 min read

What happened in Kryvyi Rih

Russian drones struck a shopping centre in the central Ukrainian city of Kryvyi Rih on 21 August, killing at least 15 people and injuring more than 130, according to reports from Reuters and the Associated Press citing Ukrainian authorities. Children were among the injured. Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelenskyy described the attack as a double-tap strike, meaning a second wave followed the first while rescuers and civilians were already responding.

The incident was not isolated. AP reported that separate Russian attacks in the Kharkiv and Mykolaiv regions also caused deaths on Friday, including children in Mykolaiv. The scale and timing of the attacks matter because they fit a wider pattern this summer: large drone and missile barrages are becoming a routine part of the war even when the front line itself moves only slowly.

Important: casualty figures can change after publication as hospitals and emergency services update records. This article uses figures reported by Reuters and AP at the time shown in the source list.

The air war is becoming more central

Ukraine's air force said it intercepted or suppressed 107 of 135 Russian drones launched overnight, according to AP. That claim cannot by itself show how effective every interception was, but it illustrates the volume of incoming attacks Ukraine is trying to absorb. Kyiv has repeatedly asked partners for more air-defence interceptors, particularly systems capable of countering ballistic missiles.

Ukraine is also pushing its own long-range campaign deeper into Russia. AP reported Ukrainian claims that drones hit an oil refinery in Perm and a military airfield in the Volgograd region. Russian officials, for their part, reported intercepting hundreds of Ukrainian drones. The exact damage from individual strikes can be difficult to independently verify quickly, but the strategic direction is clear: both sides are trying to impose costs far from the immediate battlefield.

Why diplomacy is not matching the battlefield tempo

The military escalation is happening alongside diplomatic language that remains far apart. Russian Deputy Foreign Minister Sergei Ryabkov told Reuters that Moscow was prepared to consider new ideas for ending the war, but only if they reflected what Russia calls the realities on the ground. That formulation points directly to the territorial dispute at the heart of the negotiations.

Washington continues to say it wants to mediate an end to the war, while warning that a quick settlement is unlikely. European leaders are also preparing another meeting of the coalition supporting Ukraine. Those diplomatic channels matter, but for now they have not produced a ceasefire capable of reducing the pace of strikes.

What to watch next

The next phase will likely turn on three connected questions. First is whether Ukraine receives enough air-defence ammunition to reduce the impact of repeated Russian barrages. Second is whether Ukraine's expanding long-range campaign can materially disrupt Russian logistics, energy infrastructure or military operations without driving a larger cycle of retaliation. Third is whether outside mediators can narrow the gap over territory and security guarantees enough to restart meaningful negotiations.

For civilians, those strategic questions are immediate rather than abstract. The Kryvyi Rih attack shows how quickly a normal urban space can become part of the battlefield, even hundreds of kilometres from the most contested front-line sectors.
