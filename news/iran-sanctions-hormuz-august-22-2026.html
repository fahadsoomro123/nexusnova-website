Sanctions and Hormuz disruption move to the centre of the Iran conflict

Direct U.S.-Iran fire is quieter for the moment, but the conflict is increasingly being fought through sanctions, oil flows and control of one of the world's most important shipping routes.

Published 22 Aug 2026NexusNova Editorial DeskApprox. 6 min read

A conflict entering a different phase

Nearly six months into the U.S.-Iran conflict, the immediate focus has shifted from major direct exchanges toward economic pressure and control of trade. Reuters reported on 22 August that Washington and Tehran were still trading hostile rhetoric ahead of a new round of U.S. sanctions expected to be detailed on Monday. Reuters also reported that there was no active fire between the two countries at the time of that update.

That does not mean the conflict is settled. The unresolved dispute is now heavily concentrated around Iran's oil revenue, the Strait of Hormuz and the conditions for any renewed diplomacy. Those issues can affect countries far beyond the region because Hormuz is a critical route for oil and liquefied natural gas exports.

Oil exports are becoming a pressure point

Reuters reported that offers of Iranian oil to Chinese buyers have fallen sharply as the U.S. blockade and sanctions campaign tighten. According to trade-source data cited by Reuters, Iranian shipments fell to about 534,000 barrels per day in August from an average of roughly 1.4 million barrels per day in 2025. China remains the dominant buyer of shipped Iranian crude, making Chinese purchasing decisions central to how much pressure Tehran actually feels.

Fewer available barrels have also changed pricing. Iranian crude that once tended to sell at a discount has reportedly become harder to obtain, pushing some buyers toward alternatives from countries such as Iraq and Brazil. That is a sign that sanctions are not only a political tool; they are reshaping physical trade routes.

Why this matters globally: disruption around Hormuz can feed into crude prices, shipping costs, inflation and government budgets even in countries that do not buy Iranian oil directly.

Hormuz remains the strategic hinge

Ship-tracking data reported by Reuters showed only nine commodity vessels passing through the Strait of Hormuz on both Tuesday and Wednesday this week, an unusually low level compared with normal trade. A separate Reuters market report said seven commodity ships passed on Thursday. Tracking data is imperfect because some vessels may switch off transponders, but the direction of travel is clear: commercial movement through the strait remains constrained.

That makes Hormuz more than a military chokepoint. It is leverage. Iran's ability to disrupt or threaten shipping raises the economic cost of prolonged confrontation, while U.S. pressure on Iranian exports targets Tehran's ability to finance the state and sustain the conflict.

The human impact is increasingly economic

Associated Press reporting from Iran describes households struggling with rising prices, job losses and a weakening currency. Economic damage does not stay confined to government balance sheets; it changes what families can buy, how businesses operate and how much room leaders have to absorb further pressure.

That pressure can cut in opposite directions politically. It may increase incentives for negotiation, but it can also harden public attitudes if sanctions are viewed as collective punishment or external coercion. That is one reason economic warfare does not automatically produce a diplomatic breakthrough.

What to watch next

Monday's expected sanctions announcement will be the next obvious signal. The key question is not just how broad the measures are, but whether Washington can persuade major trading partners to enforce them. Watch Chinese oil purchases, vessel traffic through Hormuz and any sign that mediators can reopen a negotiating channel.

The most important indicator may be whether both sides begin describing a realistic off-ramp rather than simply raising the cost of staying in the confrontation. Without that, a quieter military moment can still remain economically dangerous and politically unstable.
