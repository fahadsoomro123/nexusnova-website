New strikes expose the gap between a ceasefire and a settlement

The ceasefire has reduced the scale of fighting, but renewed Israeli strikes and a basic disagreement over disarmament and withdrawal are keeping a durable political settlement out of reach.

Published 22 Aug 2026NexusNova Editorial DeskApprox. 6 min read

Why the ceasefire is under pressure

Israeli strikes in Gaza this week have renewed questions about how far the current ceasefire can move from reduced fighting toward a lasting agreement. The Associated Press reported at least 17 deaths in strikes across Tuesday and Wednesday, citing local hospitals, the Palestinian Red Crescent and Gaza authorities. Reuters separately reported at least 16 Palestinians killed since Tuesday when mediators Turkey, Egypt and Qatar issued a joint warning that the violence could undermine efforts to advance the ceasefire.

Israel's military said several of the targets were Hamas commanders or militants, including individuals it accused of taking part in the 7 October 2023 attack. Palestinian officials reported that police personnel and civilians were also among those killed. Those accounts are not identical, which is why the details need to remain attributed rather than compressed into a single unqualified version.

Context: the ceasefire that began in October 2025 significantly reduced the intensity of the war, but it did not eliminate strikes, deaths or disputes over movement, security and political control.

The central argument is about sequence

The biggest diplomatic obstacle is no longer simply whether disarmament and withdrawal should happen. It is which should happen first. Hamas has said it is prepared to begin disarming under conditions that include an Israeli halt to strikes and withdrawal, with further steps tied to a broader Palestinian political settlement. Israeli Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu has said Israel will not withdraw from its current positions until Hamas is fully disarmed.

That gap may sound procedural, but it is fundamental. Each side sees the other's first step as the security guarantee for its own. Without a sequencing mechanism both accept, even a detailed reconstruction or security plan has difficulty moving from paper to implementation.

Mediators are warning about lost momentum

Turkey, Egypt and Qatar — all involved in mediation — said the latest strikes threaten talks at a critical stage. Their intervention came after another round of U.S.-led diplomacy failed to produce a visible breakthrough. Reuters reported that Washington has nevertheless committed more than $206 million toward a proposed International Stabilization Force and related infrastructure for Gaza.

That funding shows that outside actors are planning for a post-war security structure even while the core political conditions remain unresolved. Reuters reported that five countries had agreed to contribute troops to the proposed force, while Egypt and Jordan were expected to support police training. But a stabilization force cannot substitute for agreement between the parties on withdrawal, disarmament and governance.

Humanitarian recovery still depends on political stability

For more than two million people in Gaza, the difference between a fragile ceasefire and a durable one affects far more than military risk. Reconstruction, reliable aid flows, medical access, housing and basic services all depend on predictable security and functioning governance. Even limited renewed violence can disrupt that planning and create new uncertainty for civilians.

This is also why individual casualty figures, while important, do not tell the full story. The broader question is whether the ceasefire architecture is strong enough to absorb violations and disputes without collapsing into a larger round of fighting.

What to watch next

The clearest signal of progress would be a negotiated sequence that links verified disarmament steps to verified Israeli withdrawals, rather than asking one side to complete its entire obligation first. Watch also for whether mediators can establish stronger monitoring, whether attacks decrease after their latest warning, and whether the proposed international force develops a mandate accepted by the key parties.

Until those pieces move together, Gaza is likely to remain in an unstable middle ground: less intense than full-scale war, but still far from a settled peace.
