How percentage calculations work

To find a percentage of a number, multiply the number by the percentage and divide by 100. To find what percent A is of B, divide A by B and multiply by 100. Percentage change compares the difference with the starting value.

Three worked examples

Percentage of a number: 15% of 2,500 is 375 because 2,500 × 15 ÷ 100 = 375. What percent is A of B: 30 is 20% of 150 because 30 ÷ 150 × 100 = 20. Percentage change: moving from 80 to 100 is a 25% increase because the change of 20 is divided by the starting value 80.

Common uses

Percentages are useful for discounts, marks, growth, price changes, commissions and everyday comparisons. Use the first calculator when you know the rate, the second when you are comparing a part with a whole, and the change calculator when you want to measure movement relative to a starting value.

Important edge cases

A percentage change needs a non-zero starting value because division by zero is undefined. A move from 0 to a positive number therefore cannot be expressed with the ordinary percentage-change formula. Negative starting values can also make percentage-change interpretation unintuitive, so for finance, scientific or statistical reporting, check whether a simple percentage change is the right measure before publishing the result.

Results may contain decimal values. The calculator keeps several significant decimal places for practical use, but financial or regulatory calculations can have their own rounding rules. Apply the rounding convention required by the context rather than assuming every result should be rounded the same way.

Percentage calculations: formulas and principles

The calculator uses three standard relationships. To find p percent of x, use result = (p * x) / 100. To find what percent a part is of a whole, use percent = (part / whole) * 100, provided the whole is not zero. For percentage change from an old value to a new value, use change% = ((new - old) / old) * 100, provided the old value is not zero. The sign matters: a positive result indicates an increase and a negative result indicates a decrease under this convention.

Practical use cases

Use the first formula for discounts, tax-rate estimates or exam marks; use the second when comparing one quantity with a total; and use percentage change when reviewing prices, traffic, project costs or other measurements over time. Example: 15% of 2,500 is 375. Example: 30 is 20% of 150. Example: moving from 80 to 100 is a 25% increase. These examples also show why the denominator must match the question being asked.

Privacy and local processing

This calculator performs its arithmetic directly in the browser. Entered numbers remain in the page's browser memory for the calculation and are not sent as calculator inputs to an external server. The tool has no required account or file-upload step for these calculations. Static page resources still load normally from the website, but the numerical inputs used for a result are processed locally.

Edge cases and interpretation

Reading percentage results correctly

Percentage points and percent change are different statements. A rate moving from 10% to 15% changes by 5 percentage points, but the relative change in the rate itself is 50%. Likewise, a 20% discount followed by another 20% discount is not a single 40% discount because the second reduction uses the already reduced price. Always write down the baseline and the quantity being measured so the denominator is unambiguous.

For repeated growth or decline over several periods, apply each period's rate to the updated value rather than simply adding the percentages. For example, two consecutive 10% increases produce 1.10 x 1.10 = 1.21 times the starting value, or a 21% cumulative increase. This distinction is important when comparing monthly changes, investment returns, prices or project estimates. For a multi-period forecast, identify the starting baseline for each step and keep the calculation order explicit.

Division by zero is undefined, so the calculator rejects a zero whole when finding a part's percentage and rejects a zero old value for the ordinary percentage-change formula. Negative values can be mathematically valid but may require domain-specific interpretation. Rounding also matters: financial, scientific and regulatory contexts can prescribe their own decimal precision, so treat the displayed result as a calculation aid and apply the rounding convention required by your use case.
