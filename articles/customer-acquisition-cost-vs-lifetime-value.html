Customer Acquisition Cost vs Lifetime Value

Acquisition cost measures what it takes to win a customer; lifetime value estimates the contribution that customer generates over a defined relationship.

Marketing metrics are useful only when their definitions match. Customer acquisition cost, or CAC, divides relevant acquisition spend by the number of new customers attributed to that spend. Lifetime value, or LTV, estimates the value expected from a customer over a period or relationship, and should normally account for gross margin, retention, servicing and refunds. Revenue-only LTV can make an unprofitable business look healthy when fulfilment costs are high.

Formula and definitions

Formula: CAC = attributable acquisition cost ÷ new customers; LTV should be based on contribution, not just revenue

Keep acquisition costs in the numerator

Include the cost categories selected by the metric definition: paid media, creative, affiliate commissions, sales labour or marketing software. State whether overhead and existing staff time are included. Use the same campaign window for spend and attributed customers, and distinguish new paying customers from leads, trials or account sign-ups. If customers arrive after a long sales cycle, the attribution period may need to reflect that delay.

Estimate value from retained contribution

A useful LTV model begins with contribution per period after variable fulfilment and servicing costs, then applies realistic retention or churn assumptions. Contract length, purchase frequency, returns, discounting and expansion revenue can all matter. A simple average may work for a stable cohort, but different customer segments can have very different behaviour. Label whether the figure is observed historical value, a forecast, or a gross revenue proxy.

Read the ratio together with payback

An LTV-to-CAC ratio compares expected customer contribution with acquisition cost, but it does not show when cash returns. A long payback period can strain a business that has limited cash even if the ratio eventually looks attractive. Review monthly contribution, refund rates, time to first purchase and the cash needed to serve the customer before scaling a campaign. Cash runway and profitability are linked but not interchangeable.

Test sensitivity before spending more

Model what happens if conversion falls, advertising becomes more expensive or customers churn earlier than expected. Use conservative values for newer cohorts where there is little retention history. Monitor results by channel and segment rather than relying on a blended ratio that hides poor-performing acquisition sources. Do not optimize a metric by excluding reasonable costs or defining a customer after seeing the outcome.

Worked example

A campaign spends $12,000 and generates 300 new paying customers, so CAC is $40. If the average customer contributes $18 a month after variable costs and the realistic paid lifetime is eight months, modeled contribution LTV is $144 before additional fixed costs and discounting. The simple ratio is 3.6 to 1. If retention falls to four months, contribution LTV falls to $72 and the ratio becomes 1.8 to 1. The retention assumption is doing real work in the result.

Common mistakes

Do not compare revenue LTV with fully loaded CAC without explaining the mismatch. Do not mix cohorts, currencies or time windows. Do not claim a universal healthy ratio: acceptable economics depend on gross margin, payback, capital costs and the business model. Use a cohort’s observed results as evidence, not a guarantee that future buyers will behave the same way.

Next steps

Create a metric dictionary before comparing marketing channels. Record included costs, new-customer definition, cohort period, contribution margin, retention assumptions and payback. Revisit the estimate when cohort behaviour changes and use downside scenarios for budget approvals. The metric should inform a decision, not replace the decision-maker’s review of cash needs and risk.

Frequently asked questions

Should LTV use revenue or profit?

Contribution after variable costs is usually more decision-useful; state the chosen definition.

Can a high LTV-to-CAC ratio still be risky?

Yes. Slow payback, volatile retention, platform dependence and cash constraints can create risk even with a high projected ratio.
