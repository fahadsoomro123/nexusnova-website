MPG vs. L/100 km: Compare Fuel Economy Across Units

Convert the fuel-economy units correctly, then compare how much fuel vehicles use over the same distance and what that fuel costs.

Fuel economy can be shown as distance per unit of fuel (miles per gallon, or MPG) or as fuel used per distance (litres per 100 kilometres, or L/100 km). These scales move in opposite directions: higher MPG means more distance per gallon, while lower L/100 km means less fuel consumed over the same distance.

Check which gallon the MPG figure uses. US and imperial gallons are different sizes, so a vehicle's US MPG number cannot be compared directly with an imperial MPG value without conversion. Also compare like-for-like real-world use, since speed, traffic, temperature, load, tyres and driving style affect the result.

Formula and inputs

US gallon conversion: US MPG = 235.215 ÷ (L/100 km) and L/100 km = 235.215 ÷ US MPG.

Imperial gallon conversion: Imperial MPG = 282.481 ÷ (L/100 km) and L/100 km = 282.481 ÷ Imperial MPG.

The conversion constant depends on the gallon definition. Use positive fuel-economy values only; a zero MPG input cannot be used as a divisor, and negative economy figures do not represent normal fuel consumption.

Worked example

A vehicle rated at 8 L/100 km converts to 235.215 ÷ 8 = about 29.4 US MPG. The same consumption converts to 282.481 ÷ 8 = about 35.3 imperial MPG. These are two ways to express the same underlying fuel use, not a change in the vehicle's efficiency.

To compare annual fuel cost, suppose Vehicle A uses 8 L/100 km and Vehicle B uses 9.5 L/100 km. At 15,000 km per year, A uses 15,000 ÷ 100 × 8 = 1,200 litres; B uses 15,000 ÷ 100 × 9.5 = 1,425 litres. At an illustrative $1.60 per litre, annual fuel costs are $1,920 and $2,280 respectively. A uses 225 fewer litres and saves about $360 per year at that price, before any difference in purchase price, maintenance or driving conditions.

The estimate changes with annual distance, actual consumption and fuel price. Compare vehicles over the same distance and use the same fuel-price assumption; otherwise a difference in cost may come from the scenario rather than the vehicle.

How to apply the result

  1. Record the economy figure and confirm whether MPG is US or imperial; keep L/100 km values labelled as consumption, not distance per gallon.
  2. Convert both vehicles to a common fuel-economy unit if you want to compare the rates directly.
  3. Estimate annual fuel use as annual kilometres ÷ 100 × L/100 km. If you start with miles, convert distance to kilometres before applying that formula.
  4. Multiply litres by the same per-litre price for both vehicles. In the United States, alternatively calculate US gallons used and multiply by a per-US-gallon price.
  5. Test realistic annual-distance, fuel-price and consumption scenarios; do not assume a rated figure will match every route or driver.

Common mistakes

  • Mixing US and imperial gallons: the constants differ, so confirm the unit printed by the source.
  • Thinking higher L/100 km is better: it means more fuel is needed for the same distance.
  • Comparing different annual distances: use the same driving distance and fuel-price assumption.
  • Treating lab ratings as guaranteed real-world use: route, temperature, traffic and load can change consumption.
  • Confusing fuel cost with total vehicle cost: purchase price, insurance, taxes and maintenance are separate parts of an ownership comparison.

Frequently asked questions

Why does lower L/100 km mean better efficiency?

L/100 km measures how many litres are required to travel 100 kilometres. If a car needs 7 litres instead of 9 litres for that distance, it uses less fuel. MPG measures the inverse relationship, so the better result is a higher MPG number.

Is US MPG the same as imperial MPG?

No. An imperial gallon is larger than a US gallon. For the same L/100 km figure, the imperial MPG number is higher; use 235.215 for US MPG and about 282.481 for imperial MPG in the conversions above.

How do I estimate annual fuel cost from L/100 km?

Multiply annual kilometres by L/100 km and divide by 100 to estimate annual litres. Then multiply those litres by the fuel price per litre. For example, 15,000 km at 8 L/100 km uses 1,200 litres; at $1.60 per litre that costs $1,920.
