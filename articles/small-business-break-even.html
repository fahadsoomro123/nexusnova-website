Small-Business Break-Even: Calculate the Sales You Need

Estimate the units needed to cover fixed costs and stress-test price, variable costs and demand.

A break-even calculation estimates the sales level at which contribution from sales exactly covers the fixed costs included in the model. It is the no-profit, no-loss point for those assumptions, not a sales target that automatically produces a healthy cash position. Separate unit break-even from the sales revenue required to reach it, and check how far forecast sales sit above or below that level.

This article focuses on unit volume, target-profit volume and margin of safety. Use one consistent time period and define what is included in variable cost, fixed overhead and owner compensation. Financing costs, taxes, replacement equipment and cash collection timing may require an additional layer beyond this simplified operating calculation.

Formula and inputs

Contribution per unit: selling price per unit − variable cost per unit

Break-even units: fixed costs ÷ contribution per unit. Round up to the next whole unit when the result is not an integer.

Break-even revenue: break-even units × selling price per unit, or fixed costs ÷ contribution-margin ratio when the sales mix and contribution-margin ratio are reasonably stable.

Units for a target operating profit: (fixed costs + target profit) ÷ contribution per unit.

Margin of safety: forecast sales − break-even sales. Its percentage form is (forecast sales − break-even sales) ÷ forecast sales × 100% when forecast sales are positive.

Worked example

Assume monthly fixed costs are $6,000, the selling price is $50 per unit and variable cost is $30 per unit. Contribution is $50 − $30 = $20. Break-even volume is $6,000 ÷ $20 = 300 units, and break-even revenue is 300 × $50 = $15,000.

If the business forecasts 360 units, forecast revenue is $18,000. The margin of safety is 360 − 300 = 60 units, or 60 ÷ 360 = 16.7% of forecast unit sales. Under this simplified model, expected operating profit is (360 × $20) − $6,000 = $1,200 before any excluded costs.

If the owner instead wants $2,000 in operating profit, target volume is ($6,000 + $2,000) ÷ $20 = 400 units, equal to $20,000 in revenue at the same $50 price. These figures are only useful while price, unit mix and variable cost assumptions remain valid.

How to apply the result

  1. List monthly fixed costs, including an appropriate owner-pay allowance when the model is intended to show a sustainable operating target.
  2. Estimate variable cost per unit for materials, payment processing, packaging, shipping or labour that varies with output.
  3. Calculate unit contribution and break-even units; use a consistent product or service mix if different offerings have different margins.
  4. Convert the result to revenue and calculate the margin of safety against a realistic sales forecast.
  5. For a target profit, add that amount to fixed costs before dividing by contribution. Stress-test lower price, higher variable costs and lower demand.
  6. Compare the result with actual sales and cash collections each month. Treat unpaid invoices and excluded cash expenses separately so a break-even profit estimate is not mistaken for available cash.

Common mistakes

  • Calling break-even a profit goal: the model covers defined costs but produces no operating profit at the break-even point.
  • Using revenue instead of contribution: variable costs must be deducted before sales contribute to fixed costs.
  • Ignoring sales mix: if products have different prices and margins, one unit assumption may not represent the actual mix.
  • Using a forecast without a margin of safety: small demand changes can push sales below break-even.
  • Confusing modeled profit with cash: payment delays and excluded expenses can still cause a shortfall.

Frequently asked questions

Does break-even include business profit?

No. Break-even is where contribution exactly covers the fixed costs included in the model, so the calculated operating profit is zero at that point. To plan for a profit target, add the target amount to fixed costs before dividing by contribution per unit.

How much should I sell above break-even?

There is no single suitable margin of safety for every business. Compare the realistic sales forecast with break-even volume and test whether the remaining gap can absorb a slower month, price discounts or higher variable costs.

Can I use the formula for several products?

Only with care. If prices and contribution margins differ, the result depends on the expected sales mix. Use a weighted contribution margin based on a defensible forecast mix or calculate scenarios for the main products rather than treating every unit as identical.

Does break-even show whether I have enough cash?

Not by itself. It is an operating-cost calculation and may exclude payment delays, debt payments, taxes, equipment purchases and other cash timing issues. Compare it with a separate cash-flow forecast and the costs relevant to the decision.
