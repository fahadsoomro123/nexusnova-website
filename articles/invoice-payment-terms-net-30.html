Net 30 Invoice Terms Explained

Net 30 generally means payment is due 30 days after the contractual starting event, subject to the invoice and agreement.

An invoice term affects when a supplier expects cash, but it is not cash in the bank. Contracts can measure 30 days from invoice date, delivery, acceptance or a separate milestone, so do not assume the trigger without checking the agreed language. Weekends, public holidays, dispute processes and local law may affect the exact due date. Consistent terms reduce confusion, but written acceptance and a clear payment workflow matter just as much as the label.

Formula and definitions

Formula: Expected cash date = contractual due-date trigger + agreed payment period, adjusted only as the contract or law permits

Write the trigger and due date clearly

State whether the period begins on invoice date, receipt, completed delivery or accepted work. Include the due date where practical so the customer does not have to infer it. Make sure purchase orders, master agreements and invoices do not conflict. For cross-border customers, clarify currency, bank details and who pays transfer fees. Ambiguous terms invite disagreements over when a payment is late.

Plan receivables using realistic timing

A net 30 invoice may be paid earlier, on time or later, depending on customer process. Forecast expected cash using payment history and dispute status rather than assuming every invoice settles exactly on its due date. Large customers can have fixed payment runs, while a new customer may need onboarding or supplier registration. Track invoice sent date, acceptance, due date, outstanding balance and next follow-up in one receivables list.

Use follow-up without surprising the customer

Send a correct invoice with purchase-order references and supporting documents the customer needs. Confirm receipt, then send a concise reminder before and shortly after the due date. If an invoice is disputed, isolate the disputed portion and resolve the documentation gap. Late fees and suspension rights must be agreed and permitted by applicable law; do not add a charge after the fact and assume it is enforceable.

Negotiate terms against working capital

Longer payment terms may win a customer but require the supplier to finance wages, stock and tax obligations while waiting. Compare expected gross margin with the cost of capital, concentration risk and days sales outstanding. For smaller suppliers, a deposit, milestone payment or shorter term may be more important than a slightly higher price. Offer predictable paperwork and payment options without sacrificing clarity around acceptance and due dates.

Worked example

A service invoice is dated March 2 with an agreement stating that net 30 runs from invoice date. If the contract does not modify the calendar rule, the nominal due date is 30 days later, around April 1 depending on the year and how days are counted in the agreement. If the contract instead starts the clock when work is accepted on March 9, the due date moves later. The invoice should state the agreed trigger, not invent one.

Common mistakes

Do not treat a net term as a promise that the money will clear on the due date. Do not ignore contract precedence, acceptance milestones, banking delays or an invoice dispute. Do not assume late charges are lawful or that a reminder replaces a required formal notice.

Next steps

Use a written template with the exact trigger, due date, currency, payment method and contact for disputes. Add invoice reminders to a cash calendar and forecast customer delays conservatively. When a payment becomes overdue, preserve a paper trail and confirm the next action under the signed agreement and applicable law.

Frequently asked questions

Does net 30 always mean 30 days after the invoice date?

No. The contract may define a different trigger such as delivery or acceptance.

Can I charge late fees automatically?

Only where contract terms and applicable rules permit them; confirm the requirements first.
