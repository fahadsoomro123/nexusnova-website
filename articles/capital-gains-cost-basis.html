Capital Gains and Cost Basis: Record the Real Gain

Estimate a basic gain using net sale proceeds and adjusted basis, then organise records for tax review.

Tax rules, thresholds and filing duties depend on the jurisdiction and tax year.

Before calculating, record the asset type, acquisition date, purchase amount, transaction costs, later basis adjustments, units sold, sale date and net proceeds. Basis rules can differ for shares, property, gifts, inherited assets and business property, so keep the supporting records for the rule that applies to this asset.

Formula and inputs

Formula: Gain = net sale proceeds − adjusted cost basis

Use proceeds and adjusted basis for the same asset quantity and currency. Subtract eligible sale costs only where the applicable tax rules allow them, and calculate a partial sale separately from the unsold holding. Keep the gain calculation distinct from the taxable gain and the final tax owed.

Worked example

Suppose an asset sells for $5,250 and a $50 transaction fee leaves $5,200 net proceeds. If the adjusted cost basis is $4,500, the basic gain is $5,200 − $4,500 = $700 before any other adjustment or tax treatment. This is the gain calculation, not the amount of tax owed.

Adjusted basis may differ from the original purchase price. Depending on the asset and local rules, transactions such as stock splits, reinvested distributions, improvements, return-of-capital payments, gifts or inheritance can change the basis or the method used to determine it. Keep supporting records and confirm which adjustments apply to your asset.

Important limitation: Tax rules, thresholds, holding-period definitions and filing requirements depend on the jurisdiction, asset and tax year.

How to apply the result

  1. Identify the asset, account type, acquisition date and disposition date.
  2. Find the original purchase price or other starting basis in the relevant records.
  3. Apply documented basis adjustments only when they apply to the asset and jurisdiction; do not assume every listed event changes basis the same way everywhere.
  4. Subtract transaction costs that properly reduce proceeds under the applicable rules to estimate net proceeds.
  5. Subtract adjusted basis from net proceeds to calculate the basic gain or loss, then separately check the applicable tax treatment, holding-period rules and reporting requirements.
  6. Retain statements, receipts, fee records and adjustment documentation so the calculation can be reproduced during tax preparation.

Common mistakes

  • Using sale price instead of net proceeds: reconcile transaction costs where local rules allow them to reduce proceeds.
  • Assuming purchase price is always adjusted basis: certain asset events or acquisition methods may change the basis.
  • Treating gain as tax owed: taxable gain and tax liability are not the same calculation.
  • Assuming every asset uses the same tax rate: treatment can depend on asset type, holding period, account type, jurisdiction and year.
  • Losing supporting records: keep acquisition, sale and basis-adjustment documentation together.

Frequently asked questions

Is every realised gain taxed at the same rate?

No universal rate applies to every asset or taxpayer. The treatment may depend on jurisdiction, asset type, holding period, account type, other income and special rules. Use the gain formula to estimate the amount first, then consult current official guidance for the tax treatment that applies.

Is a capital gain the same as tax owed?

No. Gain is the difference between net sale proceeds and adjusted basis in this simplified calculation. Tax owed depends on the applicable taxable-gain rules, any offsets or exclusions, the taxpayer's circumstances and the relevant tax year.

Why might adjusted basis differ from purchase price?

Some events can change basis or the method used to determine it. Possible examples include stock splits, reinvested distributions, improvements, return of capital, gifts or inherited assets, but the rules are asset- and jurisdiction-specific. Use the records and guidance that actually apply to your situation.

What records should I keep?

Keep purchase or acquisition records, sale confirmations, transaction costs, statements showing reinvested distributions or other adjustments, and any documentation supporting a gift or inherited basis. Retention periods and reporting obligations depend on the applicable local rules.
