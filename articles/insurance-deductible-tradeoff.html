Insurance Deductible vs. Premium: Compare the Trade-Off

Compare premium savings with a higher deductible and check whether the additional out-of-pocket amount is affordable.

A higher insurance deductible usually trades a lower premium for a larger out-of-pocket cost when a covered claim occurs. The simple break-even calculation compares the annual premium saving with the increase in deductible, but it does not predict whether or how often you will make a claim.

Compare quotes for the same policy type, coverage limits, exclusions and term so the deductible is the main difference. Then test both the long-run premium arithmetic and whether you could pay the higher deductible from available cash after an unexpected loss.

Formula and inputs

Deductible increase: higher deductible − lower deductible

Annual premium saving: lower-deductible premium − higher-deductible premium

Simple break-even years: deductible increase ÷ annual premium saving, when the premium saving is positive.

This is a simplified claim-free comparison. It assumes the quoted premiums and coverage stay unchanged, and ignores the timing of savings, claim frequency, taxes or policy details. If the higher-deductible premium is not lower, this break-even formula does not indicate a savings advantage.

Worked example

Suppose the lower-deductible quote has a $500 deductible and a $1,200 annual premium. A comparable quote with a $1,100 deductible costs $1,020 per year. The deductible increases by $600 and the premium falls by $180 per year, so the simple break-even period is $600 ÷ $180 = 3.33 years.

In three claim-free years, the premium saving totals $540, still less than the $600 deductible increase. After four claim-free years, it totals $720. But for an otherwise identical covered claim large enough to reach both deductibles, the higher-deductible policy could leave up to $600 more out of pocket. A claim can therefore offset several years of premium savings; repeated claims may change the result again.

The exact difference for a smaller loss depends on the claim amount and policy terms. A loss below the higher deductible may produce no payment under that policy even when the lower-deductible option would pay something. Check how the policy applies the deductible to each covered event.

How to apply the result

  1. Get quotes for the same policy type, coverage limits, exclusions and term, changing the deductible rather than comparing unrelated policies.
  2. Record the annual premium and deductible for each quote; convert monthly premiums to a common annual period if needed.
  3. Subtract the premiums to calculate the yearly saving and subtract the deductibles to calculate the extra out-of-pocket amount.
  4. Divide the deductible increase by the positive annual saving. Treat the result as a simple comparison horizon, not a claim forecast.
  5. Check if you could pay the higher deductible promptly without missing rent, food, utilities or other essential expenses. Consider how multiple claims and policy-specific limits would change the trade-off.

Common mistakes

  • Using mismatched quotes: different coverage or exclusions make the premium difference hard to attribute.
  • Assuming the break-even date predicts claims: it only compares cumulative premium savings with the deductible increase under simplified assumptions.
  • Ignoring repeated claims: a deductible may apply to each covered event under the policy wording.
  • Choosing a deductible that cannot be paid: the premium saving is not useful if a claim would cause an immediate cash crisis.
  • Assuming all losses are covered: exclusions, coverage limits and claim requirements can determine whether a payout occurs.

Frequently asked questions

Does a higher deductible always save money?

No. It can lower the premium, but a covered claim may cost more out of pocket. In the example, the annual saving is $180 while the deductible increases by $600, so it takes about 3.33 claim-free years to offset that difference under the simple assumptions.

What does the break-even period actually mean?

It is the time required for cumulative premium savings to equal the increase in deductible, assuming premiums remain the same and no claim changes the comparison. It is not a prediction of when a claim will happen or a complete expected-cost calculation.

Should I pick the higher deductible if I cannot afford it today?

Do not judge the option by premium alone. If paying the higher deductible would jeopardise essential expenses, the cheaper premium may not be worth that cash-flow risk. Compare the policy terms and the amount you could realistically access after a covered loss.
