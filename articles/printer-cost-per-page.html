Printer Cost per Page: Compare Ink, Toner and Maintenance

Estimate the cost of each usable printed page, including consumables, paper and service parts, then compare total cost at your expected volume.

Printer cost per page is more than the price of an ink cartridge or toner divided by its advertised yield. A useful estimate includes the consumables actually used, paper and any separately replaced maintenance parts. It also distinguishes rated yield under a stated test pattern from the number of pages your own documents can produce.

Compare printers using the same page mix, colour coverage, paper size, print volume and cost categories. Manufacturer yield figures are useful starting points, but large images, high coverage, cleaning cycles, calibration, duplex printing and discarded pages can change real costs.

Formula and inputs

Consumable cost per page: consumable price ÷ usable page yield

Paper cost per printed page: paper cost per sheet × sheets used per page. For single-sided pages this is usually one sheet per page; duplex output may use about half a sheet per page on average, depending on the job.

Estimated running cost per page: consumables per page + paper per page + separately billed maintenance per page

Annual running cost: cost per page × annual printed pages. Add a reasonable share of the printer purchase price if you want annual total ownership cost, and account separately for electricity or service charges where material.

Worked example

A $90 toner cartridge is rated for 3,000 pages, which is $90 ÷ 3,000 = $0.03 (3 cents) per rated page before paper or maintenance. If your page coverage and waste mean you get about 2,400 usable pages, toner cost becomes $90 ÷ 2,400 = $0.0375 (3.75 cents) per usable page.

Assume paper costs $5 for 500 sheets, or $0.01 per sheet, and separately purchased maintenance parts cost $120 over 10,000 pages, or $0.012 per page. Using one sheet per printed page, the estimated running cost is $0.0375 + $0.01 + $0.012 = $0.0595, or 5.95 cents per page. At 4,000 pages a year, that is about $238 a year in running costs, excluding the printer purchase price, electricity and any costs not listed.

This is a worked illustration, not a universal adjustment factor for rated yields. Replace the usable-yield assumption with your own print mix or observed cartridge/page records. Only include a drum or other maintenance part as a separate cost if it is not already included in the consumable price.

How to apply the result

  1. Record the exact printer and consumable model numbers, cartridge price and the rated yield basis (such as black-only or colour).
  2. Estimate usable pages for your real mix of text, graphics, colour coverage and cleaning/waste rather than assuming the advertised yield is guaranteed.
  3. Convert paper packs to cost per sheet, and convert any separate drum, printhead or maintenance kit into a per-page amount using its estimated service yield.
  4. Multiply the running cost per page by the pages you actually expect to print each year. Keep paper sheets and printed sides separate when duplexing.
  5. For a purchase decision, add purchase price and material energy or service costs over a common ownership period; apply the same assumptions to every printer in the comparison.

Common mistakes

  • Dividing by rated yield and calling it actual cost: coverage, cleaning and waste can reduce usable pages.
  • Counting only ink or toner: paper and separately replaced service parts may also matter.
  • Double-counting maintenance: check whether a drum or printhead is already bundled in the cartridge price.
  • Confusing printed pages with sheets: duplex printing can use fewer sheets than printed sides.
  • Ignoring device cost at low volume: include purchase and relevant running costs when comparing ownership options.

Frequently asked questions

Why is the real cost per page higher than rated yield?

Rated yield is measured under a specified test method and document coverage. Colour graphics, images, cleaning cycles, calibration, waste and your actual print mix may use more consumable per usable page. Track actual cartridge changes and page counts if you need a closer estimate.

Should I count a separate drum or printhead?

Include it when the part is purchased separately and is not already included in the ink or toner price. Divide its price by a realistic estimate of the pages it will serve, and apply the same accounting rule to each printer being compared.

How do I compare a low-volume printer with a high-volume model?

Use the same expected annual page count and page mix. Add consumables, paper, separate maintenance and a consistent share of purchase price over the same ownership period. A low running cost per page does not automatically make the more expensive printer cheaper when you print very little.
