CAGR vs. Average Annual Return: Compare Growth Honestly

Calculate compound annual growth and see why arithmetic average returns can misstate the growth experience.

CAGR describes the constant annual growth rate that would connect a starting value to an ending value over a period. An arithmetic average return simply averages periodic percentage returns. They answer different questions, especially when returns vary from year to year.

Formula and the key distinction

CAGR: (ending value ÷ starting value)^(1 ÷ years) − 1. It captures the compounded path between two endpoints. An arithmetic average is (r1 + r2 + ... + rn) ÷ n and does not reproduce the effect of gains and losses compounding on the balance.

Worked example: why averages can mislead

Start with $100. A 20% loss produces $80. A 25% gain then brings the balance back to $100. The arithmetic average of −20% and +25% is +2.5%, but the two-year CAGR is 0%. The average sounds positive while the investor has not grown the original balance at all.

Practical workflow

  1. Record the starting and ending values and exact dates.
  2. Check whether deposits, withdrawals, fees or taxes occurred between those dates.
  3. Use CAGR for a clean endpoint-growth comparison when external cash flows do not distort the endpoints.
  4. Use a suitable cash-flow-aware method when money entered or left the account during the period.

Common mistakes

  • Calling the arithmetic average a growth rate: it may not describe compounded wealth.
  • Ignoring cash flows: deposits and withdrawals can distort endpoint comparisons.
  • Ignoring fees and taxes: gross and net growth answer different questions.
  • Treating historical CAGR as a forecast: past growth does not guarantee future returns.

Frequently asked questions

Does CAGR show volatility?

No. Two investments can have the same CAGR while following very different paths. Review the sequence of returns and risk separately.

Can CAGR be used when there are deposits?

Only with care. External cash flows mean the endpoint change is not solely the investment's performance, so a cash-flow-aware return calculation may be more appropriate.
