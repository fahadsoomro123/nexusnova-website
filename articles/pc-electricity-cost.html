PC Electricity Cost: Estimate a Computer’s Power Bill

Estimate energy and cost from actual PC and monitor draw, the hours each device is used, and the applicable per-kWh rate.

To estimate a PC's electricity cost, use the power it draws from the wall during real use, not just the wattage printed on its power supply. A power supply's rating describes its output capacity; actual draw changes with the CPU/GPU workload, system components, power settings and conversion efficiency. A monitor and other peripherals add their own consumption.

Work, gaming and idle use can have very different power levels. Estimate them separately when possible, and use a wall energy meter rated for the equipment or a reliable measured kWh value. Include the monitor, speakers, network equipment or other devices only if you intend to include them in the same estimate.

Formula and inputs

Energy per mode: kWh = average wall watts ÷ 1,000 × hours used

Daily PC energy: sum of each mode's kWh

Daily energy cost: total kWh × marginal tariff per kWh

Monthly estimate: daily kWh × days used × tariff, provided the same rate applies to those units. Fixed bill charges, taxes and higher tariff slabs may need separate treatment.

Worked example

Suppose the PC averages 250 W at the wall and runs for five hours per day. It uses 250 ÷ 1,000 × 5 = 1.25 kWh per day. At $0.25 per kWh, the variable energy charge is 1.25 × $0.25 = $0.3125, or about $0.31 per day for the PC alone.

If a 35 W monitor is used for the same five hours, it adds 35 ÷ 1,000 × 5 = 0.175 kWh per day. Combined use is 1.425 kWh per day, or about $0.36 a day. Over 30 days, that is 42.75 kWh and approximately $10.69 at the example rate, before fixed charges, tax or tariff tiers.

These figures are illustrative, not a standard cost for every PC. Replace the average wall draw, hours and tariff with your own measurements and bill rate. If the PC sleeps overnight or is left on for long idle periods, count those hours at an appropriate idle or sleep draw instead of assuming full-load consumption.

How to apply the result

  1. Measure the PC at the wall during realistic work, gaming and idle states; do not treat the PSU's maximum rating as constant consumption.
  2. For each state, record average watts and hours per day. Calculate each state's kWh and add them together.
  3. Measure the monitor and other peripherals separately if their cost should be included. Avoid counting any device twice if it is already in the measured combined draw.
  4. Multiply total kWh by the incremental tariff that applies to your usage. Add fixed charges and taxes only when estimating the whole bill rather than the PC's variable energy cost.
  5. Compare the estimate with metered usage over a representative week or month, allowing for other loads and changes in tariff slabs.

Common mistakes

  • Using PSU capacity as actual draw: a 750 W rating does not mean the computer continuously consumes 750 W.
  • Ignoring the display: a monitor adds to total cost if it is not included in the measured draw.
  • Assuming gaming draw all day: use separate realistic estimates for heavy use, regular work, idle and sleep.
  • Applying the average household rate blindly: slabs, peak rates and fixed charges can make marginal cost different.
  • Using an unsuitable meter or extension lead: only use equipment rated for the load; do not open mains-powered equipment to take measurements.

Frequently asked questions

Can a 750 W power supply consume 750 W all day?

It can supply up to its rated output under suitable conditions, but that rating is not a constant wall-draw figure. A typical PC's actual input changes with workload and component demand, and the wall draw includes power-supply conversion losses. Measure the system at the wall if you need a more accurate cost estimate.

Should I include the monitor and router?

Include them when estimating the whole workstation's electricity cost. Measure each device separately or use a combined meter reading, but do not add a device's watts again if it was already part of the combined measurement.

Why is my measured cost different from this example?

Average power, gaming or idle hours, sleep settings, peripheral use and the applicable tariff all vary. A full bill may also include other appliances, taxes, fixed charges or higher energy tiers. Compare a representative meter period and separate the PC's consumption from the whole-house total.
