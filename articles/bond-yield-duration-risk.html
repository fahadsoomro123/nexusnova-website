Bond Yield and Duration: Separate Income from Price Risk

Yield describes income relative to a bond price; modified duration estimates how that price may respond when market yields move.

Bond yield and modified duration answer different questions. Yield describes income relative to a bond's price under a stated yield convention; modified duration estimates the bond price's percentage sensitivity to a small change in market yield. A higher yield does not automatically mean a better risk-adjusted choice, because price volatility, credit quality, reinvestment, liquidity and when the money is needed also matter.

Duration is not simply the number of years until maturity. It is a sensitivity measure derived from the bond's cash flows and yield. The estimate is most useful for relatively small yield changes; larger moves, embedded calls, changing credit spreads and convexity can cause actual price changes to differ.

Formula and inputs

Approximate percentage price change: − modified duration × yield change (decimal) × 100

Express the yield change as a decimal: a rise of one percentage point is 0.01, not 1. Duration of 5 multiplied by 0.01 and then by 100 gives an estimated price change of −5%. The minus sign captures the usual inverse relationship: when market yields rise, existing fixed-rate bond prices generally fall; when yields fall, prices generally rise, all else equal.

Use modified duration for the matching bond or portfolio and a yield change in the same convention. The estimate is a rate-sensitivity scenario, not a forecast of total return or a guarantee of what the bond can be sold for.

Worked example

Suppose a bond holding is worth $10,000 and has modified duration of 5. If its relevant market yield rises by one percentage point (0.01), the approximate price change is −5%, or around −$500, leaving an estimated market value of about $9,500 before convexity, credit-spread moves and other effects. A one-point yield decline would give an approximate +5% move under the same simplified assumption.

The yield received is not the same as that price change. Coupon income may partly offset a price decline during a holding period, but the final return also depends on purchase price, time held, reinvestment rates, default or downgrade risk, fees and taxes. Avoid adding a yield figure to the duration estimate unless the return periods and assumptions are aligned.

How to apply the result

  1. Record the bond price, yield convention, maturity, coupon type and modified duration from a current bond quote or trusted provider.
  2. Choose a yield scenario in percentage points, convert that change to a decimal and apply the duration formula to estimate a price move.
  3. Test both rising and falling yields, especially if the position may need to be sold before maturity.
  4. Review call features, credit quality and likely cash-needed date; those risks are not captured by the duration calculation alone.
  5. For a portfolio or large yield move, treat this as a first-pass estimate and use the provider's fuller pricing analysis, which may include convexity and spread changes.

Common mistakes

  • Entering 1 instead of 0.01 for a one-percentage-point change: yield changes must be expressed as decimals.
  • Reading yield as guaranteed total return: price changes, default, fees, taxes and reinvestment affect the outcome.
  • Treating duration as maturity: duration measures sensitivity; maturity is a contractual date.
  • Assuming duration captures every risk: credit spreads, calls, liquidity and convexity may change the result.
  • Comparing bonds with different conventions: align yield definitions, dates and cash-flow assumptions.

Frequently asked questions

Does holding a bond to maturity remove every risk?

No. If the issuer pays as promised, holding an individual bond to maturity can reduce the need to realise an interim market-price loss. It does not remove default risk, inflation risk, reinvestment risk, call provisions, taxes or the cost of needing to sell early. Fund shares also do not have one maturity date for the investor in the same way an individual bond does.

Does a high yield mean a bond is better?

Not by itself. A higher quoted yield can reflect greater credit risk, a falling market price, a longer duration or terms that require closer review. Compare yield convention, duration, issuer risk and when you may need the money.

How do I read a duration of 5?

As a first approximation, a 1-percentage-point yield rise (0.01) suggests about a 5% price decline: −5 × 0.01 × 100 = −5%. Actual movement may differ because of convexity, spread changes, embedded options and other market factors.
