Refinancing Break-Even: Is the New Loan Worth It?

Estimate how long upfront refinance costs take to recover, then check whether the new loan still makes sense over the time you expect to keep it.

Refinancing break-even is a first-pass estimate of how long it takes for recurring payment savings to recover the upfront costs of a new loan. It is not a complete comparison of borrowing cost: a lower payment can result from extending the repayment term, and the new loan may leave a different balance at the date you sell or refinance again.

Use itemised, comparable loan estimates. Keep closing costs paid in cash separate from costs added to the new loan balance, and compare the current and proposed loans over the same expected holding period. The result depends on the actual contract, rate type, remaining term, fees and payment schedule.

Formula and inputs

Monthly payment reduction: old monthly payment − new monthly payment

Simple break-even months: upfront refinance costs ÷ monthly payment reduction, when the reduction is positive.

Use costs actually borne by the borrower, including points and lender or closing charges that apply; do not assume rolled-in costs are free just because they are not paid at closing. If the payment reduction is zero or negative, this simple payment-savings formula has no positive break-even period. Also compare the remaining principal balances, because equal payment savings can conceal different payoff paths.

Worked example

Suppose the new loan has $3,000 in refinance costs and reduces the monthly payment by $125. The simple break-even is $3,000 ÷ $125 = 24 months.

If you sell or refinance again after 18 months, the payment savings would total $125 × 18 = $2,250, leaving $750 of upfront costs unrecovered under this simplified measure. At 30 months, savings total $3,750, which exceeds the $3,000 cost. Neither result by itself proves the refinance is better: any difference in the outstanding principal balance, interest paid, rolled-in fees or loan term can change the overall comparison.

For example, stretching a remaining 20-year repayment schedule into a new 30-year loan can reduce the required monthly payment while potentially increasing the time interest is paid. Compare the total payments and estimated loan balance at the date you expect to sell or pay off the loan, not only the headline installment.

How to apply the result

  1. Obtain itemised estimates for the existing loan and the proposed refinance. Match the coverage, rate conventions and loan assumptions as closely as possible.
  2. List costs paid at closing, costs added to the loan, points and any applicable prepayment penalty; avoid counting one fee twice.
  3. Subtract the new monthly payment from the old payment. If the result is not positive, do not claim a payment-based break-even.
  4. Calculate the simple break-even, then compare it with the number of months you realistically expect to keep the loan.
  5. For that same future date, compare cumulative payments plus upfront costs and the remaining principal balance for both loan paths. Include rate resets and term differences that apply under the actual documents.
  6. Recheck the result if the expected move date, sale date, future rate or closing quote changes. A lender's Loan Estimate and closing disclosures should take precedence over an illustrative example.

Common mistakes

  • Choosing only by installment: a lower payment does not automatically mean lower total cost.
  • Ignoring the expected holding period: savings that take 24 months to recover are less useful if you leave after 18 months.
  • Forgetting rolled-in fees: they increase the loan balance even when they are not paid upfront.
  • Ignoring remaining principal: different terms can leave different balances at the same future date.
  • Comparing different loan terms as if equal: align the time horizon and include rate resets or prepayment rules where relevant.

Frequently asked questions

Is a lower payment enough to justify refinancing?

No. Check upfront costs, how long you expect to keep the loan, the new term, and the remaining balance at the same future date. A smaller monthly payment can still cost more overall if repayment is stretched out or fees are added to the balance.

What does a 24-month break-even mean?

It means cumulative monthly payment reductions equal the upfront cost after 24 months under the simplified assumptions. It does not include every difference between the two loan balances, total interest, taxes or contract terms.

What if I expect to move before break-even?

Compare the estimated payment savings over the months you expect to stay with upfront costs and the balance you would owe at that time. In the example, 18 months of $125 savings totals $2,250, which is $750 less than $3,000 in costs before considering loan-balance differences.

Should fees added to the loan be ignored in break-even?

No. Rolled-in fees increase the amount borrowed and can affect interest and the remaining balance. Include them in the new loan's balance and cash-flow comparison even though they were not paid in cash at closing.
