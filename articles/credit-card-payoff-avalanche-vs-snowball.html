Credit Card Payoff: Avalanche vs. Snowball Method

Compare the debt avalanche and snowball methods, organize minimum payments, and build a realistic credit-card payoff plan.

Make a plan from the balances and APRs you actually have

Three credit cards, three due dates, and one extra $250 a month can feel harder to manage than one large loan. Before choosing a strategy, list each card's balance, annual percentage rate (APR), minimum payment, due date, and whether a promotional rate is about to expire. The plan should cover every minimum payment first. Late fees and penalty rates can undermine progress before the extra payment even reaches principal.

The Consumer Financial Protection Bureau explains two common approaches: prioritize the highest interest rate, or prioritize the smallest balance. The first generally aims to minimize interest cost; the second creates earlier visible wins. Its overview is available in the CFPB debt-reduction guide.

Debt avalanche: highest APR first

Pay the minimum due on every card, then direct the remaining payoff money to the card with the highest APR. When that account is paid off, move its former payment to the card with the next-highest APR. This method typically reduces interest compared with paying extra on a lower-rate balance while a higher-rate balance remains, assuming other terms and payment timing are comparable.

The avalanche can feel slow when the highest-rate card also has the biggest balance. Track progress using principal reduction or the interest avoided, not only the number of accounts closed. If two rates are close, a tiny mathematical advantage may matter less to long-term success than choosing a plan you can consistently follow.

Debt snowball: smallest balance first

Pay minimums on all cards and put extra money toward the smallest balance. After that balance reaches zero, roll the payment into the next-smallest balance. Removing an account can simplify the calendar and create momentum. The trade-off is that a larger, high-interest balance may keep accumulating interest while the smaller card is being cleared.

Example: choosing between two strategies

CardBalanceAPRMinimum
A$65024%$35
B$2,40019%$80
C$1,10029%$45

For avalanche, card C is the first target because it has the highest APR. For snowball, card A is first because its balance is smallest. Both plans still pay the minimum on the other two cards. Do not treat the table as an interest calculation; actual interest depends on the card agreement, average daily balance, new purchases, fees, and payment timing.

Prevent new charges from erasing progress

Where practical, stop putting everyday spending on a card you are trying to clear. If a full stop is not realistic, track new purchases separately and make sure they are not larger than the extra principal payment. Build a small cash buffer for common surprises so a car repair or medical bill does not immediately send the balance back up. Contact the issuer early if minimum payments are becoming unmanageable; ask about available hardship options rather than waiting until an account is delinquent.

Make the monthly amount sustainable

Calculate what remains after housing, utilities, food, transportation, insurance, required payments, and a realistic allowance for irregular bills. Use only the amount that can be repeated, not an optimistic figure that relies on zero unexpected expenses. When income varies, choose a baseline payment and treat occasional surplus as extra rather than making a large payment that leaves no cash for next week's groceries.

Common questions

Which method saves more money?

With similar fees and terms, paying the highest APR first usually minimizes interest. A precise comparison should model balances, rates, minimum formulas, and future payments.

Should I close a paid-off card?

Not automatically. Consider fees, spending control, credit utilization, account age, and your broader credit profile before making that decision.

What if the minimums are unaffordable?

Prioritize essential needs, contact issuers, review the full budget, and consider reputable nonprofit credit counseling. Avoid promising yourself a payoff schedule that requires missing other essential bills.
