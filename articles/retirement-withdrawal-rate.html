Retirement Withdrawal Rate: Stress-Test Income, Not Just a Percentage

Estimate a starting withdrawal rate and stress-test inflation, fees, market decline and retirement length.

A retirement withdrawal rate compares the annual amount you plan to take from an investment portfolio with the portfolio's starting value. It is a useful planning ratio, not a forecast of investment returns or a promise that savings will last for life. The result depends on how withdrawals change over time, the portfolio mix, market returns, inflation, costs, taxes and how long the money must support spending.

Start with the amount the portfolio needs to provide, after considering other reliable income such as Social Security or a pension. Then distinguish between a fixed-dollar strategy that raises withdrawals with inflation and a percentage-of-current-balance strategy that lets the cash amount change with the portfolio.

Formula and inputs

Initial withdrawal rate: first-year annual portfolio withdrawal ÷ starting investable portfolio × 100%

Use assets genuinely available to fund retirement spending, not an inaccessible home value unless you have a realistic plan to use it. Keep the spending amount and portfolio value on the same date and currency basis. Consider whether the withdrawal amount is before or after taxes and fees.

Two strategies that are easy to confuse

  • Fixed-dollar, inflation-adjusted method: choose an initial withdrawal, then adjust that dollar amount for inflation in later years. The rate is calculated from the starting portfolio; it is not recalculated as the same percentage of each new balance.
  • Percentage-of-current-balance method: withdraw a chosen percentage of the portfolio's current value each period. The dollar amount falls after market losses and rises after gains, so spending is less predictable but withdrawals respond to the balance.

These strategies answer different questions. Do not describe one method as the other, and do not assume a popular starting percentage automatically accounts for a longer retirement, taxes, fees or your personal spending pattern.

Worked example

A $24,000 first-year withdrawal from a $600,000 starting portfolio is $24,000 ÷ $600,000 = 4%. With a fixed-dollar strategy and 3% inflation, the next year's planned withdrawal would rise to $24,720.

Now stress-test an early decline. If the portfolio value fell to $480,000 before that next withdrawal, taking $24,720 would equal 5.15% of the reduced balance. That does not mean the plan has automatically failed, but it shows why early losses combined with ongoing withdrawals can put more pressure on the remaining portfolio. A percentage-of-current-balance strategy would instead take a smaller dollar amount after the decline if it kept the same rate.

The figures are illustrative and exclude investment returns after the stated balance, fees and taxes. They show the arithmetic difference between the two approaches, not a recommended rate for an individual household.

How to apply the result

  1. Estimate essential and discretionary annual spending, then subtract the income sources expected to cover part of that budget.
  2. Choose a consistent starting date and estimate the portfolio assets available for withdrawals.
  3. Calculate the initial rate from the first-year portfolio-funded amount divided by the starting portfolio.
  4. Compare at least one fixed-dollar, inflation-adjusted path with a percentage-of-current-balance path. Write down how spending would change after market losses.
  5. Stress-test early declines, inflation, lower future returns, ongoing fees, account-specific taxes and a longer retirement horizon.
  6. Review the plan at least annually and after major changes to spending, health, market conditions or other income sources. Use the exercise to compare assumptions, not to claim a guaranteed outcome.

Common mistakes

  • Calling 4% a guarantee: it is a rule of thumb tied to assumptions, not a contractual income promise.
  • Confusing the two methods: an inflation-adjusted dollar withdrawal is not the same as withdrawing the same percentage of the current balance each year.
  • Ignoring early losses: withdrawing during a decline can leave fewer assets participating in a recovery.
  • Using portfolio size instead of spending need: pension or benefit income can affect how much must come from investments.
  • Forgetting taxes and costs: the gross amount withdrawn may exceed the spendable amount.

Frequently asked questions

Does a 4% withdrawal rate guarantee lifetime income?

No. It is a starting rule of thumb, and the result depends on the investment mix, return sequence, inflation, costs, taxes, retirement length and spending changes. Historical or modelled success does not guarantee an individual outcome.

Is taking 4% of my current balance the same as the 4% rule?

No. The commonly described fixed-dollar method starts with a percentage of the initial portfolio and then raises the dollar withdrawal with inflation. Taking a fixed percentage of the current balance instead makes income rise and fall with the portfolio value.

Should I include Social Security or pension income?

Estimate the annual spending need that must actually come from the portfolio after other income, and consider how taxes and benefit timing affect the available amount. Do not count the same income twice or assume every withdrawal account has the same tax treatment.

How often should I revisit my withdrawal plan?

Review the plan at least annually and whenever spending, other income, health or portfolio circumstances change materially. Focus on assumptions and scenarios rather than reacting to each daily market move.
