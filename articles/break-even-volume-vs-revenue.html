Break-Even Volume vs Break-Even Revenue

Break-even units tell you how much you must sell; break-even revenue tells you the sales value required to cover the modeled costs.

A business can hit a revenue target and still miss its operating break-even point if the sales mix is too low-margin. Conversely, a stable single-product business may get a clearer operational answer from units. The right metric depends on whether price, variable cost and product mix are stable enough for the assumption to hold.

Formula and definitions

Break-even units: fixed costs ÷ contribution per unit. Break-even revenue: fixed costs ÷ contribution-margin ratio.

Contribution per unit is selling price minus variable cost per unit. The contribution-margin ratio is contribution divided by selling price. Fixed costs are covered by contribution, not by gross revenue alone.

When break-even units is the better planning number

Use units when one product or service has a stable price and variable cost. Round up when partial units cannot be sold. Keep fixed costs, price and variable cost on the same time period. If costs step up at a production or staffing threshold, recalculate the next range rather than extending the first result indefinitely.

When break-even revenue is more useful

Revenue is useful when a stable product mix makes a weighted contribution-margin ratio meaningful. If a business sells products with very different margins, the ratio should reflect realistic sales shares. A shift toward lower-margin products raises the revenue required to break even even if fixed costs do not change.

Worked example

Fixed costs are $12,000 per month. A product sells for $60 and has $36 variable cost, giving $24 contribution and a 40% contribution-margin ratio. Break-even is therefore 500 units and $30,000 revenue.

Selling price$60Per unit
Variable cost$36Per unit
Contribution$24$60 − $36
Contribution ratio40%$24 ÷ $60
Break-even500 units / $30,000$12,000 fixed costs

See how product mix changes the answer

Suppose the same business adds a second product priced at $100 with $80 variable cost. Its contribution is only $20, compared with $24 for the first product. If sales shift toward that lower-margin product, using the original 40% contribution ratio will understate the revenue needed to cover fixed costs. For a mixed business, calculate a weighted contribution ratio from expected sales shares and rerun the model when those shares change.

Break-even is not the same as a profit target

Break-even means the modeled contribution covers the included fixed costs. It does not automatically provide owner profit, debt principal, tax or a return on invested capital unless those amounts are deliberately included. For a target-profit calculation, add the required profit to the fixed-cost amount before dividing by contribution.

In the example, adding $3,000 target operating profit produces $15,000 of required contribution. That means 625 units at $24 contribution, or $37,500 revenue at the same 40% margin.

Decision checklist

  1. Choose units when one product or service has stable economics.
  2. Choose revenue when a weighted product mix is stable enough to model.
  3. Write down the period and every included fixed and variable cost.
  4. Stress-test a lower-margin or lower-volume scenario.
  5. Recalculate after major price, supplier-cost or product-mix changes.

Frequently asked questions

Why can break-even revenue change when fixed costs stay the same?

Because price, variable costs or product mix can change the contribution-margin ratio.

Can I use one product's margin for a mixed business?

Only when that product represents the stable mix being modeled. Otherwise use a weighted contribution ratio.

Does break-even include target profit?

No. Add the required profit deliberately if the goal is a target-profit model rather than simple break-even.
