Break-Even Volume vs Break-Even Revenue

Break-even units are the quantity required to cover costs; break-even revenue expresses the corresponding sales value in currency.

A business may know its break-even point in dollars but still not know how many units it must sell. The two measures are related through price, variable cost and contribution margin. A calculation should state whether it assumes one product, a stable mix of products or a service billed by hour. When product mix changes, the average contribution margin can change too, so one fixed revenue target may no longer represent the same operating risk.

Formula and definitions

Formula: Break-even units = fixed costs ÷ contribution per unit; break-even revenue = fixed costs ÷ contribution-margin ratio

Calculate contribution before choosing the metric

Contribution per unit is selling price minus variable cost per unit. The contribution-margin ratio divides contribution by selling price. Fixed costs are covered by contribution, not by gross revenue alone. A product that sells for $100 but costs $80 in variable expenses contributes $20, while another product with the same price and $40 variable cost contributes $60. Treating them as equivalent units would distort the volume calculation.

Break-even units work best for one stable item

Divide the fixed-cost amount by contribution per unit to get units needed in the selected period. Round up to a whole unit where partial units cannot be sold. Include the same period for fixed costs, price and variable cost. If some costs step up after reaching capacity, model the next range separately; the first break-even point may not remain valid beyond the staffing or production threshold.

Break-even revenue supports mixed products

For a stable product mix, the contribution-margin ratio can estimate the sales revenue needed to cover fixed costs. If the mix shifts toward lower-margin products, the old ratio overstates coverage and sales must rise. Recalculate the weighted contribution ratio from realistic sales shares and review it as pricing or channel mix changes. If a business has highly variable bundles, scenario-based volume planning may be more useful than one universal break-even revenue number.

Keep profit targets separate from break-even

Break-even means operating contribution covers the modeled fixed costs; it does not automatically provide owner profit, debt principal, tax or a return on invested capital unless those costs were included. A target-profit model adds the required profit to fixed costs before dividing by contribution. Label the analysis period and included cost set, especially when presenting a chart to lenders or investors.

Worked example

Fixed costs are $12,000 a month. A product sells for $60 and has $36 variable cost, so contribution is $24 per unit and the contribution-margin ratio is 40%. Break-even volume is 500 units; break-even revenue is $30,000. If the business wants $3,000 operating profit, the target becomes $15,000 ÷ $24 = 625 units, or $37,500 revenue at the same margin. The result changes if product mix or prices change.

Common mistakes

Do not divide fixed costs by price when variable costs are material. Do not apply a one-product formula to a changing product mix without a weighted margin. Do not omit owner compensation, required financing costs or other fixed obligations if the target is meant to represent the full operating goal.

Next steps

Create both views: units for operational planning and revenue for financial targets. State the period, product mix, contribution ratio and excluded costs. Review them when price, supplier terms or sales mix changes and use a downside scenario if sales volume is volatile.

Frequently asked questions

Why can break-even revenue change without fixed costs changing?

Price, variable costs and product mix can alter the contribution-margin ratio.

Does break-even include target profit?

Not unless the target profit is deliberately added to the cost amount in the formula.
