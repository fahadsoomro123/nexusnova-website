Water Leak Cost: Estimate the Bill Before the Next Reading

Estimate water wasted by a slow leak and use meter checks to separate continuous flow from normal use.

A small, continuous leak can waste a meaningful volume over a month, but the bill impact depends on the volume that is billed and the applicable marginal rate. Estimate the water first, convert it to the unit used on the bill, then account for water, wastewater or sewer charges and any tiered pricing that applies.

The calculation estimates the possible cost; it does not diagnose the source of a leak. You can make a few safe observations, such as reading the meter with water use paused, but avoid dismantling plumbing or equipment if you are not qualified.

Formula and inputs

Leaked volume: flow rate × duration, with matching time units.

Convert litres to cubic metres: litres ÷ 1,000 = m³.

Estimated variable water cost: billed volume × applicable marginal rate per billed unit. If wastewater charges are volume-based, estimate those separately and add them where applicable. Fixed account charges may not change with the leak volume.

Check the unit on your actual utility bill: a rate per cubic metre cannot be multiplied directly by litres, and a rate per gallon or another unit requires the corresponding conversion. Tiered tariffs may also charge different rates as total use crosses a block threshold.

Worked example

A leak of 0.1 litres per minute running continuously wastes 0.1 × 60 × 24 = 144 litres per day. Over 30 days, that is 144 × 30 = 4,320 litres, or 4.32 m³.

At an illustrative marginal water rate of $2 per m³, the water component would be 4.32 × $2 = $8.64 for those 30 days. This excludes any separate sewer/wastewater charge, fixed fee or higher tariff block. Use the rate and unit from your own bill for a local estimate.

How to apply the result

  1. Read the meter unit, billing dates, volume and variable rates from the current bill.
  2. If safe and practical, pause normal water use and compare meter readings over a measured interval; account for devices that may automatically use water.
  3. Estimate the leak's flow rate or measured volume and scale it to a day or month using the correct time conversion.
  4. Convert to the billed unit, apply the relevant marginal water rate and estimate separate volume-based wastewater charges if they apply.
  5. Compare with a prior period of similar length and note rate or occupancy changes. A higher bill total alone does not prove a leak.
  6. Use a qualified plumber or the water provider for persistent unexplained meter movement, hidden leaks or repairs beyond safe, basic checks.

Common mistakes

  • Multiplying litres by a per-m³ rate: convert litres to cubic metres first.
  • Comparing different billing periods: align dates or compare volume per day.
  • Using an average rate for every extra unit: account for the marginal rate and usage blocks.
  • Ignoring wastewater charges or fixed fees: distinguish variable leak cost from total bill components.
  • Assuming meter movement proves a specific leak: check normal use, automatic fixtures and provider explanations before diagnosing.

Frequently asked questions

Can a small leak raise a water bill sharply?

Yes, especially when it runs continuously or pushes total use into a more expensive tariff block. For example, a 0.1 L/min leak wastes about 4.32 m³ in 30 days. The bill impact depends on your provider's unit rate and any applicable wastewater or tiered charges.

How can I tell whether the meter suggests a leak?

With intended water use paused, note the meter reading and check it again after a measured interval. Automatic equipment or hidden uses may still operate, so meter movement is a clue rather than a diagnosis. If movement continues unexplained, contact your water provider or a qualified plumber.

Should I multiply leaked litres by the rate on my bill?

Only if the billed rate uses litres. If the rate is per cubic metre, divide litres by 1,000 first; for gallons or another unit, use the correct conversion. Apply the marginal rate and add any separately applicable volume-based wastewater charge.

Does the leak estimate include fixed charges?

No. A volume-based estimate measures the variable cost attributable to the extra water. Account charges that remain the same regardless of volume should be kept separate, while tiered rates or volume-based sewer fees may change the marginal cost.

This guide estimates possible consumption and cost. It is not a plumbing diagnosis; use qualified help for hidden leaks or repairs that require specialist work.
